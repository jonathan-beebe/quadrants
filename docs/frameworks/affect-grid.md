# Affect Grid

The Affect Grid is a single-item self-report instrument for measuring emotion.
It is a 9×9 grid of squares whose horizontal axis runs from **unpleasant
feelings** to **pleasant feelings** and whose vertical axis runs from
**sleepiness** to **high arousal**. The four corners are labeled with the blends
of those dimensions: **stress** (high arousal + unpleasant, top left),
**excitement** (high arousal + pleasant, top right), **depression** (low
arousal + unpleasant, bottom left), and **relaxation** (low arousal + pleasant,
bottom right). A respondent marks a single "X" in the square that best describes
how they feel at that moment; the mark's column gives a 1–9 pleasure score and
its row a 1–9 arousal score
([Russell, Weiss & Mendelsohn, 1989, full paper PDF](http://emotiondevelopmentlab.weebly.com/uploads/2/5/2/0/25200250/russell_j.a._weiss_a.__mendelsohn_g.a._1989.pdf);
[ARC instrument page, UW–Madison](https://arc.psych.wisc.edu/self-report/affect-grid-agrid/)).
It is the direct academic ancestor of the quadrant check-in tools documented
alongside it, most notably the [Mood Meter](mood-meter.md), which popularizes
the same pleasure × energy space.

## When was it created?

The Affect Grid was published in 1989 in the Journal of Personality and Social
Psychology, in the article "Affect Grid: A single-item scale of pleasure and
arousal" (vol. 57, no. 3, pp. 493–502,
[doi:10.1037/0022-3514.57.3.493](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.57.3.493);
[citation record](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=534310)).
Its theoretical basis is about a decade older: Russell's circumplex model of
affect, published in 1980, showed that emotional states can be described as
points in a two-dimensional pleasure × arousal space
([Russell, J.A. (1980), _A Circumplex Model of Affect_, JPSP, 39(6), 1161–1178](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1051239)),
and the corner labels come from Russell and Pratt's finding that rotating those
axes 45° yields the dimensions excitement–depression and stress–relaxation
([EXPRESSO method overview](https://experienceresearchsociety.org/ux-methods/affect-grid/)).

## Who created it?

[James A. Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Russell), then at the
University of British Columbia, together with Anna Weiss and Gerald A.
Mendelsohn of the University of California, Berkeley
([Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Affect-grid-:-A-single-item-scale-of-pleasure-and-Russell-Weiss/e9a2b67cc02c1fb6d2cb70a96b466988ea92cb6c),
[ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232501584_Affect_Grid_A_Single-Item_Scale_of_Pleasure_and_Arousal)).
Russell is the same psychologist whose 1980 circumplex model underlies the
[Mood Meter](mood-meter.md); the Affect Grid is his own operationalization of
that model as a measurement instrument. The provenance is unambiguous — the 1989
paper is the single primary source. Note that no standalone Wikipedia article
exists for the Affect Grid itself (only for Russell), so the citations here rest
on the paper and academic instrument databases.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Measuring mood in 1989 meant administering multi-item adjective checklists and
mood inventories — reliable, but slow. That cost is prohibitive exactly where
affect measurement is most interesting: studies that sample mood repeatedly over
time, large studies where questionnaire minutes are scarce, and designs where
filling out a long scale would itself disturb the fleeting state being measured.
Russell, Weiss, and Mendelsohn set out to build a scale "designed as a quick
means of assessing affect along the dimensions of pleasure–displeasure and
arousal–sleepiness" — a single item that captures both circumplex dimensions in
one mark
([Russell, Weiss & Mendelsohn, 1989](http://emotiondevelopmentlab.weebly.com/uploads/2/5/2/0/25200250/russell_j.a._weiss_a.__mendelsohn_g.a._1989.pdf);
[EXPRESSO method overview](https://experienceresearchsociety.org/ux-methods/affect-grid/)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It is fast and low-burden: one mark yields scores on both core dimensions of
  affect, which makes it practical for repeated measurement — before/after
  designs, experience sampling, and moment-to-moment ratings where a long
  inventory is unusable
  ([EXPRESSO](https://experienceresearchsociety.org/ux-methods/affect-grid/)).
- It is theory-grounded: the grid is a direct operationalization of the
  circumplex model, so scores map cleanly onto a large body of emotion research
  ([Russell, 1980](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1051239)).
- It was validated on publication: four studies in which students rated their
  current mood, the meaning of emotion words, and feelings conveyed by facial
  expressions showed adequate reliability plus convergent and discriminant
  validity against established multi-item scales
  ([1989 paper](http://emotiondevelopmentlab.weebly.com/uploads/2/5/2/0/25200250/russell_j.a._weiss_a.__mendelsohn_g.a._1989.pdf)).
- Honest caveats: a later independent evaluation judged it "a moderately valid,
  nonspecific measure" — fine for pleasure and arousal, but it cannot
  distinguish specific emotions (anger vs. fear) that share a region of the
  grid, and it inherits the usual limits of single-item self-report
  ([Killgore, 1998, Psychological Reports](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pr0.1998.83.2.639)).

## What is the expected outcome?

For a researcher, the outcome is a pair of interval-ish scores — pleasure 1–9,
arousal 1–9 — per respondent per moment, cheap enough to collect many times and
comparable across people, stimuli, and occasions; the Affect Grid has since been
used well beyond social psychology, for example in UX and multimedia evaluation
([EXPRESSO](https://experienceresearchsociety.org/ux-methods/affect-grid/)). For
an individual using it as a check-in tool, the outcome is the same one the
[Mood Meter](mood-meter.md) pursues: a vague felt sense becomes a concrete point
in a two-dimensional space, which is the first step toward naming and regulating
the feeling. The difference in emphasis is worth knowing — the Affect Grid is a
measurement instrument built for research speed and psychometric validity, while
the Mood Meter wraps the same space in a teaching practice; the grid gives you
numbers, the Mood Meter gives you words.
