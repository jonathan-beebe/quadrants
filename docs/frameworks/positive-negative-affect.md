# Positive & Negative Affect (Watson–Tellegen Two-Factor Model)

> **Engagement vs. distress:** the two move independently, so a flat, low-energy
> mood and an anxious, distressed one are different problems with different
> fixes.

The two-factor structure of affect — often called the **PANA model** (Positive
Activation – Negative Activation) — maps mood onto two independent axes:
**Positive Affect** (from high-PA states like _excited, enthusiastic, active_
down to low-PA states like _sluggish, drowsy, dull_) and **Negative Affect**
(from high-NA states like _distressed, nervous, hostile_ down to low-NA states
like _calm, relaxed, at rest_). Its radical claim is that these axes are largely
uncorrelated rather than opposite ends of one good–bad scale: you can be high on
both at once (fired up _and_ anxious), or low on both (placid but flat).
Crossing the axes yields four quadrants, which in Watson and Tellegen's own
circumplex correspond to the diagonal states of **Pleasantness** (high PA / low
NA), **Strong Engagement** (high PA / high NA), **Unpleasantness** (low PA /
high NA), and **Disengagement** (low PA / low NA)
([Watson & Tellegen, 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/);
[Wikipedia: Emotion classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification)).
In practice the model is used to assess mood along the two axes — most famously
via the PANAS questionnaire — in research, clinical screening, and self-report
mood tracking.

## When was it created?

The model was published in 1985, in David Watson and Auke Tellegen's review
"Toward a consensual structure of mood" (_Psychological Bulletin_, 98(2),
219–235), which reanalyzed a large body of self-rated mood studies and argued
that two broad factors — Positive Affect and Negative Affect — kept emerging as
the dominant structure
([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/);
[Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Toward-a-consensual-structure-of-mood.-Watson-Tellegen/4725e0e8e4abc698e749b30df894b06f43512bf9)).
It built directly on James Russell's 1980 circumplex model of affect, rotating
Russell's valence and arousal axes roughly 45° so that the new axes run through
the circumplex's diagonals
([Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1301428/full)).
The companion measurement instrument, the PANAS (Positive and Negative Affect
Schedule), followed in 1988
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).

## Who created it?

Psychologists
[David Watson](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Watson_(psychologist)>) and
Auke Tellegen, then at the University of Minnesota, published the model in 1985;
the marker terms drew on earlier factor-analytic work by Zevon and Tellegen
([Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule)).
Watson, Lee Anna Clark, and Tellegen together built the PANAS scales on this
foundation in 1988
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).
The underlying circular arrangement of mood terms belongs to
[James A. Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Russell) (1980), whose
circumplex Watson and Tellegen reinterpreted rather than replaced. Provenance is
well documented; note, though, that there is no standalone Wikipedia article for
the two-factor / PANA model itself — its coverage lives inside the
[Emotion classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification),
[PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule),
[Positive affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_affectivity), and
[Negative affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_affectivity)
articles.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Mood research in the early 1980s was fragmented: different studies extracted
different numbers of mood factors with inconsistent names, and existing mood
scales produced positive and negative scores whose correlations varied wildly
from study to study. Watson and Tellegen set out to find the _consensual_
structure — the factors that reliably reappear across datasets — and concluded
that two broad, largely independent dimensions did
([Watson & Tellegen, 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/)). That
independence mattered theoretically: they linked PA and NA to separate
biobehavioral systems of approach and withdrawal, implying that (for example)
low enthusiasm and high distress are distinct problems — a distinction later
used to differentiate depression (low PA) from anxiety (high NA). The 1988 PANAS
paper then solved the measurement half of the problem, selecting terms that
loaded strongly on one factor and weakly on the other to yield two brief,
"largely uncorrelated" 10-item scales
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It separates two things a single good–bad mood scale conflates: how much
  energized, rewarding engagement you feel, and how much aversive distress you
  feel. "Less stressed" and "more enthusiastic" become independently trackable
  goals.
- It legitimizes mixed states. High-PA/high-NA (keyed up before a launch) and
  low-PA/low-NA (flat but untroubled) are real, common conditions that a bipolar
  happy–sad scale cannot represent
  ([Wikipedia: Positive affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_affectivity)).
- It comes with a short, free, extensively validated instrument — the PANAS —
  making it one of the most practical affect frameworks to actually administer
  ([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/);
  [Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule)).
- One honest caveat: the axes are widely misread. "Positive Affect" is not "all
  pleasant feelings" — its low pole contains unpleasant terms like _dull_ and
  _sluggish_, and NA's low pole contains pleasant ones like _relaxed_. Watson
  and Tellegen debated Russell and Carroll through the 1990s over whether
  pleasant and unpleasant feelings are bipolar opposites; by 1999 both sides
  called it a "pseudocontroversy" — both models include a bipolar
  pleasantness–unpleasantness diagonal, and the disagreement was largely about
  rotation and terminology
  ([Russell & Carroll, 1999](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-the-bipolarity-of-positive-and-negative-affect.-Russell-Carroll/748742a6cac31b7bcb4f3e5bd506eae8397f217f);
  [Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1301428/full)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended — scoring PA and NA separately rather than computing one net
mood number — the expected outcome is a more diagnostic picture of affect. A
person or team can distinguish an energy deficit (low PA: disengaged, sluggish)
from a distress surplus (high NA: anxious, irritable) and respond differently to
each, since the interventions differ: low PA calls for adding rewarding,
approach-oriented activity, while high NA calls for reducing threat and
stressors. In research and clinical settings the model's track record is
substantial: the PANAS and its descendants (PANAS-X, I-PANAS-SF) are among the
most widely used mood measures in psychology, and the PA/NA distinction
underpins the tripartite understanding of how depression and anxiety differ
([Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule);
[Wikipedia: Negative affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_affectivity)).
For an individual using it as a quadrant check-in, the realistic outcome is the
habit of asking two questions instead of one — _how engaged am I?_ and _how
distressed am I?_ — and noticing that the answers move independently.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

The evidence base is unusually deep for a 2×2 framework, because the model ships
with a psychometric instrument that has been tested for four decades.

- **Original psychometrics.** The 1988 PANAS paper reported that the two 10-item
  scales are internally consistent, "largely uncorrelated," and stable over a
  two-month period, with strong convergent and discriminant validity against
  longer mood measures
  ([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).
- **Cross-population validation.** A systematic review of cultural adaptations
  found the PANAS retains strong psychometric properties across diverse
  populations
  ([Cultural adaptations review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370706622_A_systematic_review_of_cultural_adaptations_of_the_Positive_Affect_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule_PANAS)).
  The two-factor structure has been confirmed in children and adolescents
  ([PMC6711529](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6711529)),
  cross-national older adults
  ([older-adult validation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/314712891_Validation_of_the_PANAS_A_Measure_of_Positive_and_Negative_Affect_for_Use_with_Cross-National_Older_Adults)),
  Indonesian adolescents
  ([PMC11606227](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11606227/)), and
  Ecuadorian young adults
  ([PMC12463610](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12463610/)).
- **Clinical differentiation.** Clark and Watson's tripartite model — built on
  the PA/NA distinction — holds that high NA is shared by anxious and depressed
  patients while low PA is specific to depression, and this pattern has held in
  convergent/discriminant validity studies
  ([Watson et al., 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7897050/)). The MASQ
  (Mood and Anxiety Symptom Questionnaire) scales derived from it differentiate
  anxiety from depression with strong convergent validity
  ([MASQ review](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1591&context=psychfacpub)).
- **Known limitations.** The evidence is not uniformly favorable: critiques note
  the PANAS omits core emotions such as _joy_ while including items not usually
  considered emotions (_active, alert, strong_), contains redundant items
  (_hostile/irritable_), and under-weights cultural differences in the
  desirability of emotions
  ([Journal of Affective Disorders critique](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032713006319)).
  It captures mainly high-arousal feelings, missing low-arousal affect
  ([Psychiatry Investigation review](https://www.psychiatryinvestigation.org/m/journal/view.php?doi=10.4306/pi.2010.7.3.163)),
  and some translations show measurement-invariance problems
  ([PMC12463610](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12463610/)). The
  SPANE (Scale of Positive and Negative Experience) was developed as an
  alternative and shows incremental validity beyond the PANAS
  ([Beyond the PANAS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886915004596)).

## How do you get into the right mindset?

No formal facilitation guide exists for the model, so the orienting moves below
come from how the PANAS is administered and from the model's core insight.
Before rating anything, fix the timeframe — "right now, at the present moment"
for state affect, "in general" for trait affect — because the window determines
what you are measuring
([PhenX protocol](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/180501)).
Practitioners of the related Mood Meter add a pause first: stop and assess how
you feel in real time before plotting
([Applied Coaching Academy](https://www.appliedcoaching.org/post/the-mood-meter-for-self-awareness)).
Then orient with one question per axis and two guards against the model's common
misreadings:

- How much energized engagement do I feel — enthusiastic, alert, active — from
  none to a lot?
- How much aversive distress do I feel — nervous, irritable, upset — from none
  to a lot?
- Am I forcing the two answers to be opposites? Keyed-up-and-anxious and
  calm-but-flat are both real positions.
- Am I rating the feeling itself, or my explanation of it? Report the feeling;
  interpretation comes after scoring.

## How does one use it properly?

1. **Pick a timeframe.** The PANAS uses flexible temporal instructions — "right
   now," "today," "past week," "past month," or "in general." Momentary framing
   measures state affect; "in general" measures trait affect
   ([PhenX protocol](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/180501)).
2. **Rate the 20 items.** Rate each of 20 emotion words on a 5-point scale from
   1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). Completion takes 5–10
   minutes
   ([PANAS guide](https://positivepsychology.com/positive-and-negative-affect-schedule-panas/)).
3. **Score the two axes separately.** Sum the 10 PA items (items 1, 3, 5, 9, 10,
   12, 14, 16, 17, 19) and the 10 NA items (items 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 11, 13, 15, 18,
   20). Each score ranges 10–50. Never combine them into one net number
   ([PANAS guide](https://positivepsychology.com/positive-and-negative-affect-schedule-panas/)).
4. **Interpret against norms.** Compare with published reference means — PA
   momentary M = 29.7, weekly M = 33.3; NA momentary M = 14.8, weekly M = 17.4
   ([PANAS guide](https://positivepsychology.com/positive-and-negative-affect-schedule-panas/)).
   Read the pair as a quadrant position: high/high (keyed up), high/low (engaged
   and calm), low/high (distressed and passive), low/low (flat but untroubled).
5. **Repeat on a cadence matched to the timeframe.** Use momentary ratings for
   within-day experience sampling, weekly ratings for tracking interventions or
   team check-ins, and "in general" ratings for baseline trait assessment.
6. **Choose the right variant for the population.** PANAS-X (60 items, 11
   specific affects) for fine-grained research; the I-PANAS-SF (10 items,
   developed by Edmund Thompson for cross-national consistency) for
   cross-cultural or time-constrained settings
   ([Hogrefe: I-PANAS-SF](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/1015-5759/a000932));
   PANAS-C for children
   ([toolshero: PANAS variants](https://www.toolshero.com/psychology/panas-scale/)).

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **[Affect Grid](./affect-grid.md)** — Russell's single-item instrument for the
  same circumplex space, using the unrotated valence × arousal axes. The two are
  45° rotations of each other; the Affect Grid trades PANAS's reliability for
  speed.
- **[Mood Meter](./mood-meter.md)** — the Yale RULER tool plots the same valence
  × energy space as colored quadrants for emotional-literacy teaching; PA/NA is
  its research-grade ancestor one rotation away.
- **[Thayer Mood Model](./thayer-mood-model.md)** — Thayer's energetic and tense
  arousal dimensions correspond closely to PA and NA; the two models are often
  treated as convergent descriptions of the same structure.
- **[Dual Continuum of Mental Health](./dual-continuum-of-mental-health.md)** —
  applies the same "two independent axes, not one bipolar scale" move to mental
  illness and well-being; the PA/NA independence finding is part of its
  empirical foundation.
- **[Energy Quadrants](./energy-quadrants.md)** — Loehr and Schwartz's
  energy-management quadrants are a practitioner rendering of the same
  high/low-energy × pleasant/unpleasant space.
- **[Zones of Regulation](./zones-of-regulation.md)** — a classroom
  self-regulation curriculum whose four zones map onto the affect quadrants.
- Outside this library: Russell's 1980 circumplex model is the direct
  predecessor
  ([circumplex overview](https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/circumplex-models/));
  the tripartite model of anxiety and depression and the MASQ are direct
  clinical descendants
  ([Wikipedia: Tripartite Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_Model_of_Anxiety_and_Depression));
  Plutchik's psychoevolutionary wheel is a competing circumplex built from eight
  discrete primary emotions rather than dimensions
  ([Springer reference](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-28099-8_547-1)).

Used together, PA/NA supplies the measurement backbone: the Mood Meter or Zones
of Regulation give a fast qualitative check-in, and the PANAS quantifies the
same position when you need numbers you can track or compare against norms.

## What is the core insight?

Positive and negative affect are two independent dimensions, not opposite ends
of one scale — so engagement and distress must be measured, and addressed,
separately.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

The two-factor model itself is published scientific theory and free to use;
"positive and negative affect" is descriptive terminology with no trademark
restrictions. The PANAS instrument is copyrighted (the 1988 paper was published
in an APA journal), but the authors permit free use for non-commercial academic
and research purposes; commercial use or modification requires permission from
the copyright holders
([resref licensing guide](https://resref.com/positive-and-negative-affect-schedule-pnas-a-full-guide-for-researchers-and-clinicians/);
[ResearchGate licensing discussion](https://www.researchgate.net/post/Do_I_need_a_licence_to_use_the_Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Scale_PANAS)).
The PANAS-C (children's version) is likewise free for non-commercial use, with
commercial use requiring written permission from Watson and Clark
([PhenX: child protocol](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/180502)).
The short forms (PANAS-SF, I-PANAS-SF) are freely accessible through the PhenX
Toolkit
([PhenX: adult protocol](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/180501)).
If licensing is a concern, generic descriptions such as "two-factor affect
model" or "dimensional mood assessment" are usable, and the model's structure
can be applied without administering the copyrighted item set.

## How do you use it generically?

The restriction sits in the PANAS item set, not in the model: the two-axis
structure is published theory, so you can draw the quadrants, rename the axes,
and write your own rating words without permission.

- **Adjust the axis labels.** Affect researchers themselves recommend renaming
  the rotated axes "Positive Activation" and "Negative Activation," reserving
  "positive/negative valence" for pleasure–displeasure
  ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1301428/full)).
  The unrotated labels — valence × arousal — name the same space in Russell's
  terms
  ([circumplex overview](https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/circumplex-models/)).
- **Use generic phrases already in circulation.** "Two-factor affect model,"
  "dimensional mood assessment," and "positive/negative activation" substitute
  for the PANAS name when describing the method.
- **Swap the instrument, not the theory.** For items you can administer without
  permission, the short forms (PANAS-SF, I-PANAS-SF) are freely accessible
  through the PhenX Toolkit
  ([PhenX protocol](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/180501)), and
  the SPANE was built as an independent alternative
  ([Beyond the PANAS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886915004596)).
- **What to avoid.** Do not reproduce or modify the 20-item PANAS word list and
  its scoring key in commercial work without written permission from the
  copyright holders
  ([resref licensing guide](https://resref.com/positive-and-negative-affect-schedule-pnas-a-full-guide-for-researchers-and-clinicians/)).
