# Positive & Negative Affect (Watson–Tellegen Two-Factor Model)

The two-factor structure of affect — often called the **PANA model** (Positive
Activation – Negative Activation) — maps mood onto two independent axes:
**Positive Affect** (from high-PA states like _excited, enthusiastic, active_
down to low-PA states like _sluggish, drowsy, dull_) and **Negative Affect**
(from high-NA states like _distressed, nervous, hostile_ down to low-NA states
like _calm, relaxed, at rest_). Its radical claim is that these axes are largely
uncorrelated rather than opposite ends of one good–bad scale: you can be high on
both at once (fired up _and_ anxious), or low on both (placid but flat).
Crossing the axes yields four quadrants, which in Watson and Tellegen's own
circumplex correspond to the diagonal states of **Pleasantness** (high PA / low
NA), **Strong Engagement** (high PA / high NA), **Unpleasantness** (low PA /
high NA), and **Disengagement** (low PA / low NA)
([Watson & Tellegen, 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/);
[Wikipedia: Emotion classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification)).
In practice the model is used to assess mood along the two axes — most famously
via the PANAS questionnaire — in research, clinical screening, and self-report
mood tracking.

## When was it created?

The model was published in 1985, in David Watson and Auke Tellegen's review
"Toward a consensual structure of mood" (_Psychological Bulletin_, 98(2),
219–235), which reanalyzed a large body of self-rated mood studies and argued
that two broad factors — Positive Affect and Negative Affect — kept emerging as
the dominant structure
([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/);
[Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Toward-a-consensual-structure-of-mood.-Watson-Tellegen/4725e0e8e4abc698e749b30df894b06f43512bf9)).
It built directly on James Russell's 1980 circumplex model of affect, rotating
Russell's valence and arousal axes roughly 45° so that the new axes run through
the circumplex's diagonals
([Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1301428/full)).
The companion measurement instrument, the PANAS (Positive and Negative Affect
Schedule), followed in 1988
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).

## Who created it?

Psychologists
[David Watson](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Watson_(psychologist)>) and
Auke Tellegen, then at the University of Minnesota, published the model in 1985;
the marker terms drew on earlier factor-analytic work by Zevon and Tellegen
([Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule)).
Watson, Lee Anna Clark, and Tellegen together built the PANAS scales on this
foundation in 1988
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).
The underlying circular arrangement of mood terms belongs to
[James A. Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Russell) (1980), whose
circumplex Watson and Tellegen reinterpreted rather than replaced. Provenance is
well documented; note, though, that there is no standalone Wikipedia article for
the two-factor / PANA model itself — its coverage lives inside the
[Emotion classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification),
[PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule),
[Positive affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_affectivity), and
[Negative affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_affectivity)
articles.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Mood research in the early 1980s was fragmented: different studies extracted
different numbers of mood factors with inconsistent names, and existing mood
scales produced positive and negative scores whose correlations varied wildly
from study to study. Watson and Tellegen set out to find the _consensual_
structure — the factors that reliably reappear across datasets — and concluded
that two broad, largely independent dimensions did
([Watson & Tellegen, 1985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3901060/)). That
independence mattered theoretically: they linked PA and NA to separate
biobehavioral systems of approach and withdrawal, implying that (for example)
low enthusiasm and high distress are distinct problems — a distinction later
used to differentiate depression (low PA) from anxiety (high NA). The 1988 PANAS
paper then solved the measurement half of the problem, selecting terms that
loaded strongly on one factor and weakly on the other to yield two brief,
"largely uncorrelated" 10-item scales
([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It separates two things a single good–bad mood scale conflates: how much
  energized, rewarding engagement you feel, and how much aversive distress you
  feel. "Less stressed" and "more enthusiastic" become independently trackable
  goals.
- It legitimizes mixed states. High-PA/high-NA (keyed up before a launch) and
  low-PA/low-NA (flat but untroubled) are real, common conditions that a bipolar
  happy–sad scale cannot represent
  ([Wikipedia: Positive affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_affectivity)).
- It comes with a short, free, extensively validated instrument — the PANAS —
  making it one of the most practical affect frameworks to actually administer
  ([Watson, Clark & Tellegen, 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3397865/);
  [Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule)).
- One honest caveat: the axes are widely misread. "Positive Affect" is not "all
  pleasant feelings" — its low pole contains unpleasant terms like _dull_ and
  _sluggish_, and NA's low pole contains pleasant ones like _relaxed_. Watson
  and Tellegen debated Russell and Carroll through the 1990s over whether
  pleasant and unpleasant feelings are bipolar opposites; by 1999 both sides
  called it a "pseudocontroversy" — both models include a bipolar
  pleasantness–unpleasantness diagonal, and the disagreement was largely about
  rotation and terminology
  ([Russell & Carroll, 1999](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-the-bipolarity-of-positive-and-negative-affect.-Russell-Carroll/748742a6cac31b7bcb4f3e5bd506eae8397f217f);
  [Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1301428/full)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended — scoring PA and NA separately rather than computing one net
mood number — the expected outcome is a more diagnostic picture of affect. A
person or team can distinguish an energy deficit (low PA: disengaged, sluggish)
from a distress surplus (high NA: anxious, irritable) and respond differently to
each, since the interventions differ: low PA calls for adding rewarding,
approach-oriented activity, while high NA calls for reducing threat and
stressors. In research and clinical settings the model's track record is
substantial: the PANAS and its descendants (PANAS-X, I-PANAS-SF) are among the
most widely used mood measures in psychology, and the PA/NA distinction
underpins the tripartite understanding of how depression and anxiety differ
([Wikipedia: PANAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_Negative_Affect_Schedule);
[Wikipedia: Negative affectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_affectivity)).
For an individual using it as a quadrant check-in, the realistic outcome is the
habit of asking two questions instead of one — _how engaged am I?_ and _how
distressed am I?_ — and noticing that the answers move independently.
