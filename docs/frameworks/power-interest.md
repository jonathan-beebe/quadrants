# Power × Interest Grid

The Power × Interest grid is a 2×2 stakeholder-mapping model that classifies
stakeholders by their **power** to affect an organization or project (vertical
axis) and their level of **interest** in it (horizontal axis). Each quadrant
implies a different engagement strategy, most commonly labeled: **Manage
Closely** (high power, high interest), **Keep Satisfied** (high power, low
interest), **Keep Informed** (low power, high interest), and **Monitor** (low
power, low interest). It is the most widely used practical tool in stakeholder
analysis, appearing in strategy textbooks and project-management standards alike
([Wikipedia: Stakeholder analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stakeholder_analysis)).

## When was it created?

The idea of mapping stakeholders by power and interest traces to a conference
paper by Aubrey L. Mendelow,
["Environmental Scanning — The Impact of the Stakeholder Concept"](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis1981/20/),
presented at the Second International Conference on Information Systems in
Cambridge, Massachusetts. **A date discrepancy is worth flagging:** the paper is
almost universally cited as "Mendelow, 1991," but the AIS eLibrary hosts it in
the **ICIS 1981 proceedings** — the conference took place in 1981. The 1991 date
appears to be a citation error propagated through textbooks (notably Johnson and
Scholes' _Exploring Corporate Strategy_, which popularized the grid). The term
"stakeholder" itself first appeared in a 1963 Stanford Research Institute
internal memorandum
([Wikipedia: Stakeholder theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stakeholder_theory)).
The recognizable modern grid with named engagement strategies solidified through
the 1990s, including Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann's version in
[_Making Strategy_ (1998)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Making_Strategy.html?id=XD-zCs43eHUC).

## Who created it?

- **Aubrey L. Mendelow** (then writing in the information-systems field) is the
  standard attribution for the power–interest matrix, via the
  [ICIS 1981 paper](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis1981/20/). His paper proposed
  using the stakeholder framework — building on ideas later systematized in R.
  Edward Freeman's _Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach_ (1984) — to
  focus an organization's environmental scanning, with a contingency model
  linking scanning effort to stakeholder power.
- **Gerry Johnson and Kevan Scholes** popularized the grid for a mass audience
  in _Exploring Corporate Strategy_, which is why it is often called "Mendelow's
  Matrix" with the 1991 citation.
- **Colin Eden and Fran Ackermann** developed the grid into a hands-on group
  mapping technique in
  [_Making Strategy_ (1998)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Making_Strategy.html?id=XD-zCs43eHUC),
  naming the quadrants **Players**, **Context Setters**, **Subjects**, and
  **Crowd**
  ([Bryson 2004 discusses this lineage](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228940014_What_to_do_when_stakeholders_matter_A_guide_to_stakeholder_identification_and_analysis_techniques)).
- **Mitchell, Agle, and Wood (1997)** offered a related but distinct alternative
  — the stakeholder **salience** model, using power, legitimacy, and urgency to
  yield eight stakeholder types
  ([Wikipedia: Stakeholder analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stakeholder_analysis)).

The familiar practitioner labels (Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep
Informed / Monitor) have no single documented author; they emerged through
project-management practice and guides such as the PMBOK tradition, adapted from
Mendelow's and Johnson & Scholes' formulations.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Mendelow's original problem was **environmental scanning overload**:
organizations could not monitor everything in their environment, so he argued
the stakeholder concept provided "a basis for focusing an organization's
environmental scanning effort"
([Mendelow, ICIS 1981](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis1981/20/)) — watch most
closely the actors with the most power over you. The later strategy and
project-management adaptations solve the analogous practical problem:
stakeholder lists always exceed the time available to engage everyone (a point
Eden and Ackermann emphasize), so managers need a defensible way to prioritize
whom to engage, how deeply, and with what communication effort.

## Why should someone use it?

- It converts an unmanageable stakeholder list into **four clear engagement
  strategies**, matching effort to influence rather than treating all
  stakeholders equally.
- It surfaces **risk early**: high-power/low-interest stakeholders (Keep
  Satisfied) are the classic source of late-project ambushes, and the grid
  forces you to notice them before they activate.
- It is **cheap and fast** — a workshop whiteboard exercise — yet grounded in
  four decades of stakeholder theory, and it composes well with richer models
  (e.g., Mitchell/Agle/Wood salience) when more nuance is needed.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the outcome is a **differentiated stakeholder-engagement
plan**: key players are actively involved in shaping decisions, powerful but
distant stakeholders are kept satisfied with targeted attention, interested but
low-power stakeholders are kept informed (and often become allies), and the rest
are monitored with minimal effort. The organization spends its limited
engagement capacity where it changes outcomes, reducing the chance that a
powerful stakeholder blocks the work late, and — in Mendelow's original framing
— keeping scanning attention on the parts of the environment that can actually
hurt or help you.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

The evidence base is one of **wide institutional adoption, not controlled
effectiveness studies**:

- The grid is incorporated into the **PMBOK 7th edition** as a core stakeholder
  analysis tool in the Stakeholder Performance Domain
  ([PMBOK 7 stakeholder domain overview](https://oleg-dubetcky.medium.com/pmbok-7-project-performance-domain-stakeholders-478086aa6d23)),
  and it is described as "one of the most frequently used methods of stakeholder
  analysis" ([toolshero](https://www.toolshero.com/strategy/mendelow-matrix/)).
- Applied studies use it across domains, including scale-up of malaria
  interventions in public health
  ([European Journal of Public Health, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/eurpub/article/34/Supplement_3/ckae144.2157/7844890))
  and stadium operations management
  ([ResearchGate example](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Mendelows-power-interest-matrix-during-stadium-operations_fig1_275947011)).
- [Bryson (2004)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/pubmgr/v6y2004i1p21-53.html)
  places the grid in a peer-reviewed guide to stakeholder analysis for public
  management, arguing from case experience that such techniques help build
  winning coalitions.

No comprehensive peer-reviewed empirical validation of the grid's effect on
project outcomes was found; documented criticisms note that assessments of power
and interest are subjective, that the grid does not capture stakeholder attitude
(supportive vs. obstructive) or motivation, and that positions shift over a
project's life, requiring reassessment
([toolshero](https://www.toolshero.com/strategy/mendelow-matrix/)).

## How does one use it properly?

1. **Identify stakeholders.** Brainstorm a comprehensive list of internal and
   external parties who influence or are affected by the work; do this as a
   group so no constituency is missed
   ([Bryson 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/pubmgr/v6y2004i1p21-53.html),
   [toolshero](https://www.toolshero.com/strategy/mendelow-matrix/)).
2. **Assess each stakeholder** on the two axes: power (ability to influence
   decisions or outcomes) and interest (how much they care about the outcome).
   Mendelow identified four sources of power to weigh: possession of resources,
   ability to dictate alternatives, authority, and influence
   ([Mendelow, ICIS 1981](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis1981/20/)).
3. **Plot** each stakeholder into one of the four quadrants
   ([sopact protocol](https://www.sopact.com/use-case/stakeholder-analysis-tool)).
4. **Apply the quadrant strategy**: Manage Closely — involve in decisions and
   shape plans together; Keep Satisfied — targeted attention sufficient to keep
   them content; Keep Informed — regular communication, cultivating them as
   allies; Monitor — minimal effort, periodic checks.
5. **Set current vs. desired engagement.** PMBOK 7 layers five engagement levels
   (unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading) onto the grid; the
   engagement plan targets the gap between current and desired state
   ([PMBOK 7 stakeholder domain](https://oleg-dubetcky.medium.com/pmbok-7-project-performance-domain-stakeholders-478086aa6d23)).
6. **Reassess on a regular cadence** — at phase gates or major milestones —
   because power and interest shift over the project lifecycle
   ([toolshero](https://www.toolshero.com/strategy/mendelow-matrix/)).
7. **Pair with deeper analysis** (e.g., the salience model) when the plan needs
   stakeholder motivations and expectations, not just position.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **Stakeholder salience model** (Mitchell, Agle, Wood, 1997) — replaces the two
  axes with three attributes (power, legitimacy, urgency) yielding eight
  stakeholder types; use it after the grid when attitude and claim validity
  matter
  ([overview](https://www.stakeholdermap.com/stakeholder-analysis/stakeholder-salience.html)).
- **Freeman's stakeholder theory** (1984) — the parent theory; the grid is one
  operational tool within it
  ([_Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach_](https://www.consciouscapitalism.org/book/strategic-management-a-stakeholder-approach)).
- **Donaldson and Preston's framework** (1995) — separates stakeholder theory
  into descriptive, instrumental, and normative strands; supplies the
  theoretical grounding the grid's practical use rests on
  ([Wikipedia: Stakeholder theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stakeholder_theory)).
- **Bryson's stakeholder techniques** (2004) — embeds the grid in a fuller
  toolkit of brainstorming, influence mapping, and coalition building
  ([Bryson 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/pubmgr/v6y2004i1p21-53.html)).
- **PMBOK Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix** — extends the grid with
  current-vs-desired engagement levels
  ([PMBOK 7](https://oleg-dubetcky.medium.com/pmbok-7-project-performance-domain-stakeholders-478086aa6d23)).
- [Risk Matrix](./risk-matrix.md) — same prioritization logic (likelihood ×
  impact instead of power × interest); powerful low-interest stakeholders are
  themselves a risk worth plotting.
- [Impact / Effort](./impact-effort.md) — allocate scarce capacity by a 2×2; use
  it to prioritize the engagement actions the grid generates.
- [Eisenhower Matrix](./eisenhower-matrix.md) — the same
  attend-to-what-matters-most structure applied to tasks rather than people.
- [SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md) — complements the grid in environmental
  scanning: SWOT names the threats and opportunities, the grid names the actors
  behind them.
- [Certainty × Agreement](./certainty-agreement.md) — useful alongside the grid
  when high-power stakeholders disagree about goals or means.

## What is the core insight?

Engagement effort is finite, so match it to each stakeholder's power to affect
the outcome and interest in it, rather than treating all stakeholders equally.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

**Free to use.** No trademark is registered on "Power Interest Matrix,"
"Mendelow Matrix," or "Stakeholder Power-Interest Grid" as names for the
framework (a
[US "POWER MATRIX" registration](https://trademark.justia.com/868/97/power-86897191.html)
covers electrical surge protectors, an unrelated product). The 2×2 concept
itself carries no proprietary license; PMBOK, textbooks, and consulting firms
implement it without restriction. Equivalent names — Mendelow's Matrix,
Stakeholder Power-Interest Grid, Power vs. Interest Grid, Stakeholder Mapping
Matrix — are used interchangeably with no licensing implications. Specific book
presentations — such as Eden and Ackermann's Players / Context Setters /
Subjects / Crowd labels — are copyrighted expressions, but the underlying grid
and the generic quadrant labels are unencumbered. The framework has no dedicated
Wikipedia article; it is covered within
[Stakeholder analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stakeholder_analysis).
