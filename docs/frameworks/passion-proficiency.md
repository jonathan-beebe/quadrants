# Passion × Proficiency Audit

> **What you love vs. what you're good at:** reveals when competence, not
> passion, is deciding how you spend your week — and which work to reclaim.

The Passion × Proficiency audit is a 2×2 self-assessment for auditing how you
spend your working time. One axis is **proficiency** ("Good At It" — how capable
you are at the activity) and the other is **passion** ("Love It" — how much the
activity energizes you). The four quadrants are: **Sweet Spot** (good at it and
love it — do more of this), **Invest** (love it but not yet good at it — develop
it), **Burnout Zone** (good at it but don't love it — the dangerous quadrant,
because competence attracts more of this work), and **Avoid** (neither —
minimize, delegate, or eliminate). The intent is to notice where your calendar
actually lives versus where it should.

## When was it created?

There is no single canonical creation date, because there is no single canonical
version of this matrix. It is a folk synthesis of at least two well-dated
lineages:

- Gay Hendricks published the "four zones" (Incompetence, Competence,
  Excellence, Genius) in
  [_The Big Leap_](https://www.harpercollins.ca/9780061872785/the-big-leap/)
  (HarperOne, 2009), where the Zone of Genius is defined by the intersection of
  what you love and what you are uniquely good at.
- Marcus Buckingham's strengths work — from _First, Break All the Rules_ (1999,
  based on Gallup surveys of 1.7 million employees across 101 companies) and
  _Now, Discover Your Strengths_ (2001, with Donald Clifton) through _Love +
  Work_ (2022) — built the "love it / good at it" vocabulary, arguing that a
  true strength is an activity that both performs well _and_ strengthens you
  ([Wikipedia: Marcus Buckingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Buckingham)).

The specific 2×2 rendering with quadrant names like "Sweet Spot" and "Burnout
Zone" appears across coaching blogs and career-planning templates from roughly
the 2010s onward (e.g.
[this career-planning template](https://coda.io/@ana-garcia/the-next-step-project/make-your-passion-skills-2x2-matrix-21),
[FourWeekMBA's Zone of Genius write-up](https://fourweekmba.com/zone-of-genius/))
without a traceable first author.

## Who created it?

Honestly: **no single person**. The matrix as used in coaching today is an
unattributed folk framework. Its two clearest intellectual parents are
[Gay Hendricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_Hendricks), a psychologist
whose _The Big Leap_ (2009) popularized the enjoyment-versus-expertise zone
model, and [Marcus Buckingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Buckingham),
whose Gallup-era and later strengths research popularized separating "what
you're good at" from "what energizes you." Neither published this exact 2×2 with
these exact quadrant labels; Hendricks' zones are usually drawn as
nested/stacked zones rather than a quadrant grid. Treat any source claiming a
precise inventor of the "passion/proficiency matrix" with skepticism — we found
none with a credible citation trail. (Note: the Wikipedia article titled "The
Big Leap" is about a 2021 TV series, not Hendricks' book — there is no Wikipedia
article for the book or for this matrix.)

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Both lineages target the same trap: **competence is not the same as fulfillment,
and the world rewards you for staying in the wrong quadrant.** Hendricks called
this the "Zone of Excellence" problem — you are so good at something, and so
well rewarded for it, that everyone (including you) wants you to keep doing it,
even as it quietly drains you; his book is explicitly about escaping that zone
for the Zone of Genius
([The Big Leap](https://www.harpercollins.ca/9780061872785/the-big-leap/),
[FourWeekMBA](https://fourweekmba.com/zone-of-genius/)). Buckingham's strengths
research attacked the complementary problem: organizations obsess over fixing
weaknesses instead of deploying people where talent and energy coincide
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Buckingham)). The 2×2 audit
operationalizes both insights as a periodic self-check.

## Why should someone use it?

- It makes the Burnout Zone visible. Time diaries almost always reveal that high
  performers spend most of their week on high-proficiency/low-passion work,
  precisely because they are good at it — the matrix names that drift.
- It distinguishes two kinds of "not doing my best work": activities to _invest_
  in (energy is there, skill will follow) versus activities to _shed_ (neither
  energy nor skill), which call for opposite strategies.
- It is lightweight — a periodic 15-minute audit of actual activities, not a
  personality assessment.
- It gives vocabulary for negotiating role shape with a manager: "more Sweet
  Spot, less Burnout Zone" is a concrete, discussable request.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended — auditing real activities regularly and acting on the result —
the expected outcome is a gradual reallocation of time toward the Sweet Spot:
shedding or delegating Avoid-quadrant work, containing Burnout Zone work before
it causes actual burnout, and deliberately practicing Invest-quadrant
activities. Hendricks' claim for the equivalent move (living in your Zone of
Genius) is sustained engagement and higher-value output rather than cyclical
burnout
([The Big Leap](https://www.harpercollins.ca/9780061872785/the-big-leap/));
Buckingham's parallel claim, backed by Gallup engagement data, is that people
who use their strengths daily are markedly more engaged and productive
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Buckingham)). For this folk
2×2 specifically, no outcome research exists — the evidence base belongs to its
parent frameworks.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

**The 2×2 audit itself has no direct outcome research.** No study tests whether
plotting activities on a passion/proficiency grid improves career satisfaction,
productivity, or tenure. The evidence that exists belongs to the parent
frameworks, and it is mixed:

- **Supporting.** Gallup's strengths research base spans more than 1 million
  individuals across 50,000+ business units and reports that strengths-focused
  development correlates with higher engagement, productivity, and lower
  turnover
  ([Gallup: The Science of CliftonStrengths](https://www.gallup.com/cliftonstrengths/en/253790/science-of-cliftonstrengths.aspx)).
  The
  [CliftonStrengths technical report](https://files.wmich.edu/s3fs-public/attachments/u590/2019/cliftonstrengths_technical_report.pdf)
  documents test-retest reliability across 57,000+ respondents, while noting the
  validity evidence is strongest in U.S. corporate and educational settings.
- **Limiting.** Peer-reviewed evidence is thin: no published study demonstrates
  that strengths-based coaching outperforms other development approaches
  ([Study.com summary of Buckingham's approach](https://study.com/academy/lesson/marcus-buckingham-the-strengths-based-approach-to-management.html)).
  Center for Creative Leadership data show strengths become weaknesses when
  overused — managers who took StrengthsFinder were about five times more likely
  to overdo strength-related behaviors
  ([HBR: "Strengths-Based Coaching Can Actually Weaken You"](https://hbr.org/2016/01/strengths-based-coaching-can-actually-weaken-you)),
  and a study of 421 upper-level managers found the least effective ones
  overrated their own effectiveness
  ([Executive Coach College](https://www.executivecoachcollege.com/research-and-publications/complexity-in-strengths-based-coaching.php)).
- **Absent.** Hendricks' Zone of Genius claims have no peer-reviewed validation
  — only the book and coaching summaries
  ([The Big Leap](https://www.harpercollins.ca/9780061872785/the-big-leap/)).
  Jim Collins' adjacent Hedgehog Concept research has been criticized for
  survivorship bias and halo effects, and several of its "great" companies later
  underperformed or failed
  ([Wikipedia: Good to Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_to_Great)).

In short: large survey correlations and plausible logic, but no randomized or
controlled evidence for the audit itself. State of the evidence: suggestive, not
proven.

## How do you get into the right mindset?

Prime with recalled moments, not self-image: the audit runs on felt energy, and
labels like "I'm a builder" hide the drift it exists to catch. Warm-up questions
from the parent frameworks:

- **Passion axis.** Hendricks' prompts from _The Big Leap_: "What work do you do
  that doesn't seem like work?" and "In your work, what produces the highest
  ratio of abundance and satisfaction to the amount of time spent?"
  ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/briannawiest/2018/09/26/how-to-get-into-the-zone-of-genius-and-unlock-your-highest-potential)).
- **Proficiency axis.** "What are your proficiencies? List your skills, your
  training, things you've taught yourself. What are you really good at that
  could add value to someone else?"
  ([Calibrate360](https://calibrate360.com/how_to_discover_your_purpose/)).
- **Recency check.** Buckingham asks about last week, not your career: "Was I
  excited to work every day last week?" — and holds that loved activities need
  to stay above 20% of your work to prevent burnout
  ([How to Be Awesome at Your Job, ep. 757](https://awesomeatyourjob.com/757-how-to-find-the-career-you-truly-love-with-marcus-buckingham/)).

Before plotting, name one recurring activity you would volunteer for tomorrow
and one you would pay to hand off. If both are things you do well, you have seen
the core insight at work in your own calendar.

## How does one use it properly?

1. **Run a time audit first (one week).** Log actual activities — time, task,
   and an energy note (energized vs. drained) — rather than listing what you
   think you do
   ([TimeTackle: how to do a time audit](https://www.timetackle.com/how-to-do-a-time-audit/)).
   Audit activities at the granularity of recurring tasks ("code review",
   "status reporting", "prototyping"), not whole roles.
2. **Score each activity on both axes.** Rate proficiency on a simple scale
   (e.g. 1 = beginner to 5 = expert, as in a standard
   [skills matrix](https://www.d2l.com/blog/how-to-create-a-skills-matrix/)) and
   passion by whether the activity energizes or drains you. A binary high/low on
   each axis is enough.
3. **Plot the quadrants and weight them by hours.** The point is comparing where
   your time actually goes against where the matrix says it should.
4. **Act per quadrant.** Sweet Spot: schedule more, protect it. Invest:
   deliberate practice and stretch assignments. Burnout Zone: contain, delegate,
   or shed before it compounds. Avoid: eliminate or hand off.
5. **Negotiate the delta.** Bring the plot to a manager or peer conversation;
   "more Sweet Spot, less Burnout Zone" is a concrete request. Buckingham's
   _Love + Work_ practice is the same move made daily — noticing what you love
   inside the job you already have and weaving more of it in
   ([HBR interview](https://hbr.org/2022/04/marcus-buckingham-why-love-is-the-key-to-career-success)).
6. **Repeat on a cadence.** A 15-minute re-audit quarterly, or after any role
   change, keeps the picture current; the first pass with the time log is the
   only expensive one.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- [Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave](./love-loathe-learn-leave.md) is the closest
  sibling in this library — the same energy-versus-action logic expressed as
  four verbs rather than two axes. Run them together: this matrix locates the
  activity; that one names the move.
- [Skill × Will](./skill-will.md) is the manager-side mirror: it plots someone
  else's capability and motivation to choose a coaching style, where this matrix
  plots your own to choose a time allocation.
- [Energy Quadrants](./energy-quadrants.md) audits the same calendar by energy
  alone; pairing it with this matrix separates "draining because I'm bad at it"
  from "draining despite being good at it."
- [Competence Ladder](./competence-ladder.md) describes the path an Invest
  activity travels on its way to the Sweet Spot.
- [Importance × Satisfaction](./importance-satisfaction.md) applies the same
  gap-finding move to life domains instead of work activities.
- [SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md) shares the strengths/weaknesses axis but
  adds external opportunities and threats; useful when the audit's output feeds
  a bigger career decision.
- **Ikigai** widens the frame to four circles — what you love, what you're good
  at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for. The four-circle Venn
  diagram is a Western adaptation by Marc Winn, not the original Japanese
  concept ([Wikipedia: Ikigai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikigai)).
- **Jim Collins' Hedgehog Concept** (three circles: passion, best-in-world,
  economic engine) is the organizational-strategy cousin, from _Good to Great_
  (2001)
  ([jimcollins.com](https://www.jimcollins.com/concepts/the-hedgehog-concept.html)).
- **CliftonStrengths** (Gallup, 2001) supplies a validated instrument for the
  proficiency axis — 34 themes across four domains, taken by 26+ million people
  ([Wikipedia: CliftonStrengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CliftonStrengths)).
- **Flow theory** (Csikszentmihalyi) explains why the Sweet Spot feels the way
  it does: optimal experience arises where challenge meets skill.

## What is the core insight?

Competence attracts work regardless of whether that work energizes you, so
fulfillment requires auditing passion and proficiency as separate axes and
acting on the gap.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

The matrix itself is **free to use**. It is an unowned folk framework: no
trademark or copyright covers the 2×2 or its quadrant names (Sweet Spot, Invest,
Burnout Zone, Avoid), which are generic descriptive terms. Adjacent branded
properties are restricted:

- **CliftonStrengths®** and **StrengthsFinder** are Gallup, Inc. trademarks;
  building tools on the assessment or its 34 themes requires a written license
  ([Gallup Terms of Use](https://login.gallup.com/Home/TermsOfUse)).
- **Zone of Genius** is associated with Gay Hendricks' copyrighted book _The Big
  Leap_ (©2009 HarperOne); the phrase is widely used generically but the book's
  text and program materials are protected.
- **Hedgehog Concept** materials are Jim Collins' intellectual property
  ([jimcollins.com](https://www.jimcollins.com/concepts/the-hedgehog-concept.html)).

Generic names such as "passion/skill matrix" or "strengths and energy inventory"
carry no IP constraints, as do Ikigai, SWOT, and flow theory.
