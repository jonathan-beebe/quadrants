# Mood Meter

The Mood Meter is a 2×2 emotional-awareness tool. The horizontal axis is
**pleasantness** (how pleasant or unpleasant you feel) and the vertical axis is
**energy** (how activated or depleted your body feels). The four quadrants —
traditionally colored in the RULER program as yellow, red, green, and blue —
correspond to **Excited** (high-energy pleasant), **Tense** (high-energy
unpleasant), **Calm** (low-energy pleasant), and **Down** (low-energy
unpleasant). Users plot their current state on the grid and then, crucially,
refine the plot into a specific emotion word ("annoyed" vs. "furious"), on the
premise that granular labeling is the gateway skill to regulating emotion.

## When was it created?

In stages. The scientific foundation is James Russell's circumplex model of
affect, published in 1980, which showed that emotional states can be mapped onto
two bipolar dimensions — pleasure–displeasure and arousal–sleepiness
([Russell, J.A. (1980), _A Circumplex Model of Affect_, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 39(6), 1161–1178](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1051239);
overview in
[Psychology of Human Emotion, an open-access textbook](https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/circumplex-models/)).
A quadrant tool built on those axes was first used to help managers label
emotions by David Caruso and Peter Salovey in _The Emotionally Intelligent
Manager_ (2004); in the early 2000s Caruso and Marc Brackett reworked it into
the Mood Meter as the signature tool of the RULER program
([marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/)). A Mood Meter
mobile app followed in 2013, and the successor app "How We Feel" in 2022
([marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/)).

## Who created it?

[Marc Brackett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Brackett), founding director
of the Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence, together with colleagues — most
directly David Caruso, and building on Peter Salovey's emotional-intelligence
research — developed the Mood Meter as part of RULER, Yale's evidence-based
social-emotional learning approach
([marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/how-we-feel-app-3/),
[rulerapproach.org](https://rulerapproach.org/ruler-resources-for-families/)).
Brackett himself credits Caruso and Salovey with first using the tool in _The
Emotionally Intelligent Manager_ before he and Caruso reworked it. The
underlying two-axis structure belongs to psychologist
[James A. Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Russell), whose 1980
circumplex model the Mood Meter operationalizes (see also
[Wikipedia: Emotion classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion_classification),
which covers the circumplex model). Unlike the other frameworks in this set, the
Mood Meter has a clear, well-documented provenance.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Most people — children and adults — have an impoverished emotional vocabulary:
asked how they feel, they default to "fine," "good," or "stressed." Without
recognizing and labeling emotions accurately, people cannot understand what
caused them or regulate them, and schools in particular had no systematic way to
teach these skills. RULER (an acronym for the five skills it teaches:
Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, Regulating) was built to give
schools that system, and the Mood Meter is its core practice tool: a concrete,
repeatable way to turn a vague felt sense into a plotted point and then a
precise word
([rulerapproach.org](https://rulerapproach.org/ruler-resources-for-families/),
[marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/ruler/)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It grounds self-report in two questions anyone can answer even when they can't
  name the feeling: _how pleasant?_ and _how much energy?_ — making it unusually
  accessible for children and for adults who are alexithymic or simply out of
  practice.
- Labeling granularity is the point: moving from "bad" to "overwhelmed" or
  "resentful" changes what regulation strategy makes sense.
- It is judgment-free by design — all four quadrants are valid places to be; the
  tool asks where you are, not where you should be.
- It rests on an unusually solid research base: Russell's circumplex model
  ([1980](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1051239))
  plus two decades of RULER effectiveness studies in schools
  ([marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/ruler/)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended — checking in on the grid regularly and pushing each check-in
to a specific emotion word — the expected outcome is durable growth in the five
RULER skills: more accurate recognition of one's own states, a larger working
emotional vocabulary, and better-chosen regulation strategies. At the program
level, RULER (with the Mood Meter as its signature tool) has been adopted by
thousands of schools, with research linking it to improved emotional skills,
classroom climate, and reduced stress and burnout
([marcbrackett.com](https://marcbrackett.com/ruler/),
[rulerapproach.org](https://rulerapproach.org/ruler-resources-for-families/)).
For an individual, the honest expectation is smaller but real: a habit of
noticing and naming feelings precisely enough to respond to them deliberately
instead of being driven by them.
