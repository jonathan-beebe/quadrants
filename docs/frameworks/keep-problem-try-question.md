# Keep / Problem / Try / Question

KPT (Keep / Problem / Try) is a lightweight retrospective format, dominant in
Japan, that sorts a team's reflection into three lists: **Keep** — things that
went well and should continue; **Problem** — difficulties, friction, and
dissatisfactions worth naming; and **Try** — concrete experiments to run in the
next iteration, usually generated in response to the Problems. This app's
template adds a fourth quadrant, **Question**, for open unknowns that are
neither working nor broken — things the team wants to investigate before
deciding. A session runs left to right: everyone writes Keep and Problem items
individually, the group shares and clusters them, then brainstorms Try items and
picks a few to actually attempt; in the next retro, each Try is re-sorted into
Keep (it worked) or Problem (it didn't), making the board a running improvement
loop
([Parabol](https://www.parabol.co/templates/sprint-retrospectives/keep-problem-try/),
[Nulab](https://nulab.com/learn/project-management/run-keep-problem-try-retrospective/)).

## When was it created?

The origin is a two-step story, and neither step has a precise date. The
Keep/Problems/Try trio comes from Alistair Cockburn's **reflection workshop** in
the Crystal family of methodologies: a recurring whole-team session around a
flipchart with "Keep these", "Problems", and "Try these" areas. Cockburn
described reflection workshops on projects going back to the 1990s and
documented the practice in book form, most explicitly in
[_Crystal Clear: A Human-Powered Methodology for Small Teams_](https://books.google.com/books/about/Crystal_Clear.html?id=O_cMM5ztyMIC)
(Addison-Wesley, 2004). The acronymized "KPT" form took hold in Japan in the
mid-2000s through the **Project Facilitation** movement led by Kenji Hiranabe
and colleagues at Eiwa System Management (ESM) / ObjectClub, whose freely
licensed Project Facilitation materials — including a 67-page retrospective
("furikaeri") guide by ESM's Masaru Amano with ESM copyright dating from 2006 —
teach KPT as the standard retrospective method
([ObjectClub retrospective guide, PDF, Japanese](https://objectclub.jp/download/files/pf/RetrospectiveMeetingGuide.pdf);
[Hiranabe's Project Facilitation deck](https://www.slideshare.net/yattom/project-facilitation-from-hiranabe)).
The **Question** column is a still-later, less standardized extension with no
identifiable published origin; the analogous documented Japanese extension is
KPTA, which adds an Action column
([Agile Studio / ESM](https://www.agile-studio.jp/post/difference-try-action)).

## Who created it?

The three categories are most credibly credited to **Alistair Cockburn**
(co-author of the Agile Manifesto) via his Crystal reflection workshop, and
several template vendors state this attribution directly
([Parabol](https://www.parabol.co/templates/sprint-retrospectives/keep-problem-try/)).
The KPT branding, board layout, and enormous popularity in Japan are the work of
the Japanese agile community — most prominently **Kenji Hiranabe** and the
ObjectClub / Project Facilitation practitioners around ESM, including **Masaru
Amano**, whose guide is the fullest primary treatment
([ObjectClub PDF](https://objectclub.jp/download/files/pf/RetrospectiveMeetingGuide.pdf)).
Note an attribution conflict in secondary sources: some English-language
write-ups instead claim KPT "was first developed by Toyota"
([Nulab](https://nulab.com/learn/project-management/run-keep-problem-try-retrospective/)),
a claim we found no primary evidence for; Toyota's kaizen tradition plainly
influenced the Japanese framing of KPT as continuous improvement, but the
Toyota-as-inventor claim appears to be folklore. Who first added the
**Question** quadrant is unknown.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Cockburn's reflection workshop existed so that the team's methodology would be
adjusted by the team itself, frequently, from lived evidence — rather than
imposed once and never revisited; the flipchart categories keep that
conversation concrete and time-boxed. The Japanese Project Facilitation movement
took the practice up against a specific cultural problem: making it safe and
routine for every member — not just leads — to voice problems aloud, and turning
"hansei/furikaeri" (reflection) habits into visible, shared team artifacts in
the kaizen spirit
([Hiranabe's Project Facilitation materials](https://www.slideshare.net/yattom/project-facilitation-from-hiranabe)).
KPT's specific mechanics attack two classic retro failures: retros that are only
complaint sessions (Keep forces the positives to be recorded and protected) and
retros that end without commitments (Try forces problems to be converted into
next-iteration experiments). A Question column additionally gives uncertainty a
legitimate home, so unknowns are not forced prematurely into "problem" or
ignored.

## Why should someone use it?

It is arguably the simplest retro format that closes the improvement loop. Three
words carry the whole method; there is no metaphor to explain and no
facilitation ceremony to learn, so it works for brand-new teams and five-minute
solo reviews alike. Its distinctive strength over Start/Stop/Continue-style
formats is that the board is **stateful**: Try items from last time are
explicitly re-examined and migrate into Keep or Problem, so experiments are
actually followed up rather than forgotten
([Parabol](https://www.parabol.co/templates/sprint-retrospectives/keep-problem-try/)).
Separating Problem from Try also keeps analysis and solutioning from blurring
together — teams name what hurts before jumping to fixes
([Nulab](https://nulab.com/learn/project-management/run-keep-problem-try-retrospective/)).
The Question extension suits teams whose retros keep surfacing "we don't know
yet" items that deserve investigation rather than an immediate Try.

## What is the expected outcome?

Each session yields a preserved list of working practices, a shared honest
inventory of problems, and a small set of named experiments the team commits to
run next iteration. Repeated as intended, the categories circulate — Tries
become Keeps or new Problems, Problems spawn new Tries — producing exactly the
kaizen dynamic the Japanese community built it for: continuous, incremental,
team-owned process improvement with visible follow-through
([ObjectClub retrospective guide](https://objectclub.jp/download/files/pf/RetrospectiveMeetingGuide.pdf)).
With the Question quadrant, the team additionally leaves with an explicit list
of unknowns to investigate, preventing premature decisions on issues the team
does not yet understand.
