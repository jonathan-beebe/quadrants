# SWOT Analysis

SWOT Analysis is a strategic-planning 2×2 that assesses a person, product,
project, or organization along two axes: **internal vs. external** origin and
**helpful vs. harmful** effect. The four resulting quadrants are **Strengths**
(internal, helpful), **Weaknesses** (internal, harmful), **Opportunities**
(external, helpful), and **Threats** (external, harmful)
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis)). It is one of the
most widely used frameworks in business strategy — and, unusually for something
so ubiquitous, its true origin is genuinely contested.

## When was it created?

The framework's roots trace to the mid-1960s, but no single, undisputed creation
date exists. Two concurrent 1965 efforts are documented
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis)): at the Stanford
Research Institute's Long Range Planning Service, researchers developed a
precursor called **SOFT** analysis (Satisfactory, Opportunities, Faults,
Threats); the same year, Harvard Business School faculty published the textbook
_Business Policy: Text and Cases_, which paired the appraisal of internal
"strengths and weaknesses" with environmental "opportunities and threats."
Popular accounts also place a SOFT-to-SWOT transition at a 1964 seminar in
Zurich, where "Urick and Orr changed the F to a W"
([RapidBI](https://rapidbi.com/history-of-the-swot-analysis/)) — a story that is
frequently repeated but thinly evidenced. The acronym SWOT and the now-standard
2×2 grid presentation solidified over the following decades; the grid layout
became common considerably later than the four categories themselves.

## Who created it?

This is the contested part, and honesty requires presenting three competing
threads rather than one name:

- **Albert Humphrey / Stanford Research Institute.** The most repeated popular
  attribution credits Albert S. Humphrey, who led research at SRI in the
  1960s–70s on why corporate planning failed
  ([RapidBI](https://rapidbi.com/history-of-the-swot-analysis/)). The primary
  evidence is essentially Humphrey's own late-life account (a 2005 SRI alumni
  newsletter), and scholars regard the attribution as poorly evidenced. Notably,
  recent archival research credits SRI's SOFT framework not to Humphrey but to
  **Robert F. Stewart, Otis J. Benepe, and Arnold Mitchell**
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis), summarizing Puyt et
  al.).
- **Harvard Business School.** A long-standing academic tradition attributes
  SWOT's conceptual core to **Edmund P. Learned, C. Roland Christensen, Kenneth
  R. Andrews, and William D. Guth** and their 1965 _Business Policy: Text and
  Cases_ (some accounts push the roots back to Harvard's George Albert Smith Jr.
  and Christensen in the 1950s). A 2023 study by Richard W. Puyt and colleagues
  in _Long Range Planning_ argues that the claim SWOT was _invented_ at Harvard
  is an "academic urban legend," and credits Stanford's Long Range Planning
  Service as the true originator
  ([Puyt et al., "The origins of SWOT analysis", ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0024630123000110);
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis)).
- **No universally accepted creator.** Reviews of the framework's history
  conclude exactly this — the tool evolved across institutions and decades, and
  "there is no universally-accepted creator"
  ([RapidBI](https://rapidbi.com/history-of-the-swot-analysis/); see also
  [Rediscovering SWOT Analysis](https://scholars.fhsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1666&context=alj)).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

The 1960s corporate world had embraced long-range planning, and it was failing.
The SRI research program that produced SOFT was explicitly funded to discover
**why corporate planning failed** and to build a repeatable process executives
could actually use to set objectives and manage change
([RapidBI](https://rapidbi.com/history-of-the-swot-analysis/)). The Harvard
business-policy tradition was solving a teaching and strategy-formulation
problem: giving general managers a disciplined way to match what a company _can
do_ (internal strengths and weaknesses) with what the environment _permits and
demands_ (external opportunities and threats)
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis)). Both threads
converge on the same underlying problem: strategy decisions were being made
without a structured appraisal of internal capabilities against external
conditions.

## Why should someone use it?

SWOT forces a balanced look in four directions at once — inward at what you
control, outward at what you don't, and at both the favorable and unfavorable in
each. It is deliberately simple: it requires no data infrastructure or training,
works for a business unit, a product, a nonprofit, or a personal decision, and
is effective as a group exercise for surfacing assumptions and building shared
situational awareness
([StatPearls/NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537302/)). Its
longevity comes from that generality. Its known failure mode is equally well
documented: producing unprioritized bullet lists that lead nowhere — so it is
best used as the _input_ to strategy (e.g., matching strengths to opportunities,
planning around threats), not as the deliverable itself.

## What is the expected outcome?

A structured, shared assessment of strategic position: an explicit inventory of
internal strengths to exploit, internal weaknesses to remedy or mitigate,
external opportunities to pursue, and external threats to defend against. Used
as intended, the output feeds directly into strategy formulation — choosing
actions that apply strengths to opportunities, shore up weaknesses that expose
you to threats, and align internal capability with external reality — and gives
the team a common baseline for evaluating subsequent decisions
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis);
[StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537302/)).
