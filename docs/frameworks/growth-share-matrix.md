# Growth–Share Matrix

The Growth–Share Matrix (also called the BCG Matrix or Boston Matrix) is a 2×2
portfolio-management model that plots a company's products or business units on
two axes: **relative market share** (a proxy for competitive strength and cash
generation) and **market growth rate** (a proxy for market attractiveness and
cash consumption). The four quadrants each carry a strategic imperative:
**Stars** (high share, high growth) deserve continued investment, **Question
Marks** (low share, high growth) should be selectively funded or abandoned,
**Cash Cows** (high share, low growth) should be milked to fund the rest of the
portfolio, and **Dogs** (low share, low growth — Henderson originally called
them "pets") should be divested or repositioned
([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

## When was it created?

The matrix was introduced in 1970, when Boston Consulting Group founder Bruce
Henderson published the short essay
["The Product Portfolio"](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio)
in BCG's _Perspectives_ series. BCG's own retrospective confirms that "Bruce
Henderson devised the concept of the growth share matrix in 1970"
([BCG Classics Revisited, 2014](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)),
though the underlying portfolio thinking developed at the firm in the years just
before publication.

## Who created it?

Attribution is slightly layered, and it is worth being precise. Bruce Henderson,
BCG's founder, authored the 1970 essay and popularized the concept, and BCG
consistently describes the matrix as "developed by BCG founder Bruce Henderson."
However, the
[Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth%E2%80%93share_matrix)
records that BCG partner **Alan Zakon** (later BCG's CEO) first sketched the
matrix, refining it with colleagues before Henderson's essay spread it to the
world. BCG's 2014 retrospective also notes that Sandy Moose, who joined BCG in
1968, "worked with Bruce Henderson on the original portfolio concept." The
fairest summary: the matrix was a collaborative BCG creation, popularized and
publicly articulated by Henderson.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

The core problem was **cash allocation across a diversified portfolio**. In the
conglomerate era of the late 1960s and 1970s, business units typically kept and
reinvested their own cash, which often drove returns steadily downward. As
Henderson wrote in the 1970 essay: "A company should have a portfolio of
products with different growth rates and different market shares. The portfolio
composition is a function of the balance between cash flows" (quoted in
[BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).
The matrix gave headquarters a defensible logic for which businesses should
generate cash and which should consume it — high relative share indicated
durable cost advantage (via scale and the experience curve), while high market
growth signaled where leadership could still be won.

## Why should someone use it?

- It forces a **portfolio view**: every product is judged by its role in the
  whole (funding growth vs. consuming cash), not on its own P&L in isolation.
- It provides a **simple, communicable logic for resource allocation** —
  redeploying cash from mature winners into the businesses with the best future,
  rather than spreading investment evenly.
- It balances **exploitation of mature businesses with exploration of new
  ones**, which BCG argues is even more important in fast-changing markets,
  where the matrix works as a tool for disciplined strategic experimentation
  ([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

At the height of its influence in the late 1970s and early 1980s, roughly half
of all Fortune 500 companies used the matrix or approaches based on it.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the outcome is a **self-funding, balanced portfolio**: cash
cows finance question marks, the most promising question marks become stars,
stars mature into the next generation of cash cows, and dogs are divested before
they absorb resources. In Henderson's words: "Every company needs products in
which to invest cash. Every company needs products that generate cash… Only a
diversified company with a balanced portfolio can use its strengths to truly
capitalize on its growth opportunities." A caveat belongs here: the
[Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth%E2%80%93share_matrix)
notes academic studies finding that companies following portfolio-planning
models like the BCG matrix had _lower_ shareholder returns, and that the model
is frequently misapplied — market share is a much weaker predictor of sustained
performance today than in 1970, a point BCG itself concedes.
