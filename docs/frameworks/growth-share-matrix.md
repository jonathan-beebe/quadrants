# Growth–Share Matrix

The Growth–Share Matrix (also called the BCG Matrix or Boston Matrix) is a 2×2
portfolio-management model that plots a company's products or business units on
two axes: **relative market share** (a proxy for competitive strength and cash
generation) and **market growth rate** (a proxy for market attractiveness and
cash consumption). The four quadrants each carry a strategic imperative:
**Stars** (high share, high growth) deserve continued investment, **Question
Marks** (low share, high growth) should be selectively funded or abandoned,
**Cash Cows** (high share, low growth) should be milked to fund the rest of the
portfolio, and **Dogs** (low share, low growth — Henderson originally called
them "pets") should be divested or repositioned
([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

## When was it created?

The matrix was introduced in 1970, when Boston Consulting Group founder Bruce
Henderson published the short essay
["The Product Portfolio"](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio)
in BCG's _Perspectives_ series. BCG's own retrospective confirms that "Bruce
Henderson devised the concept of the growth share matrix in 1970"
([BCG Classics Revisited, 2014](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)),
though the underlying portfolio thinking developed at the firm in the years just
before publication.

## Who created it?

Bruce Henderson (1915–1992), BCG's founder, authored the 1970 essay and
popularized the concept, and BCG describes the matrix as "developed by BCG
founder Bruce Henderson." The
[Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth%E2%80%93share_matrix)
records that BCG partner **Alan Zakon** (later BCG's CEO) first sketched the
matrix, refining it with colleagues before Henderson's essay spread it to the
world, and BCG's 2014 retrospective notes that Sandy Moose, who joined BCG in
1968, "worked with Bruce Henderson on the original portfolio concept." In short:
a collaborative BCG creation, publicly articulated and popularized by Henderson
([Bruce Henderson — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Henderson)).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

The core problem was **cash allocation across a diversified portfolio**. In the
conglomerate era of the late 1960s and 1970s, business units typically kept and
reinvested their own cash, which often drove returns steadily downward. As
Henderson wrote in the 1970 essay: "A company should have a portfolio of
products with different growth rates and different market shares. The portfolio
composition is a function of the balance between cash flows" (quoted in
[BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).
The matrix gave headquarters a defensible logic for which businesses should
generate cash and which should consume it — high relative share indicated
durable cost advantage (via scale and the
[experience curve](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2013/growth-business-unit-strategy-experience-curve-bcg-classics-revisited)),
while high market growth signaled where leadership could still be won.

## Why should someone use it?

- It forces a **portfolio view**: every product is judged by its role in the
  whole (funding growth vs. consuming cash), not on its own P&L in isolation.
- It provides a **simple, communicable logic for resource allocation** —
  redeploying cash from mature winners into the businesses with the best future,
  rather than spreading investment evenly.
- It balances **exploitation of mature businesses with exploration of new
  ones**, which BCG argues is even more important in fast-changing markets,
  where the matrix works as a tool for disciplined strategic experimentation
  ([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

At the height of its influence in the late 1970s and early 1980s, roughly half
of all Fortune 500 companies used the matrix or approaches based on it
([Growth–share matrix — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth%E2%80%93share_matrix)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the outcome is a **self-funding, balanced portfolio**: cash
cows finance question marks, the most promising question marks become stars,
stars mature into the next generation of cash cows, and dogs are divested before
they absorb resources. In Henderson's words: "Every company needs products in
which to invest cash. Every company needs products that generate cash… Only a
diversified company with a balanced portfolio can use its strengths to truly
capitalize on its growth opportunities"
([The Product Portfolio](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio)).

## What is the hard evidence it works?

The evidence is thin, and what exists is largely negative:

- **No peer-reviewed validation.** No peer-reviewed study demonstrating that
  using the matrix improves firm performance has been identified — only
  critiques and analyses of its limitations
  ([Madsen, "Not dead yet: the rise, fall and persistence of the BCG Matrix," 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317767606_Not_dead_yet_The_rise_fall_and_persistence_of_the_BCG_Matrix)).
- **Experimental evidence against it.** In a capital-allocation experiment
  reported in that paper's review of the literature, 63.5% of participants
  exposed to the BCG matrix chose the unprofitable project
  ([criticism table, Madsen 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Criticism-of-the-BCG-Matrix_tbl2_317767606)).
- **Lower shareholder returns.** Academic studies found that companies following
  portfolio-planning models like the BCG matrix had lower shareholder returns,
  and the
  [Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth%E2%80%93share_matrix)
  records the model as "largely discredited in academic circles" — relative
  market share is a much weaker predictor of sustained performance today than in
  1970, a point BCG itself concedes.
- **Persistent practitioner use.** Despite the academic verdict, the framework
  persists in MBA curricula and consulting practice; BCG's 2014 retrospective
  argues for evolved use (shorter planning cycles, faster movement between
  quadrants) rather than abandonment
  ([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

## How does one use it properly?

Five steps, per the standard implementation protocol
([ClickUp guide](https://clickup.com/blog/bcg-growth-share-matrix/)):

1. **Select and define the unit.** Choose the business unit, brand, or product
   line to analyze; precision here prevents misclassification downstream.
2. **Define the market.** Establish market boundaries; classification depends on
   how the market is scoped.
3. **Calculate relative market share.** Divide the unit's market share by the
   largest competitor's share — a competitive-strength ratio, not absolute
   share.
4. **Calculate market growth rate.** Derive from industry reports or estimate
   from leading firms' revenue growth.
5. **Plot and assign imperatives.** Place each unit on the 2×2 plot and attach
   the quadrant's resource-allocation imperative: invest in Stars, selectively
   fund Question Marks, harvest Cash Cows, divest Dogs.

Then convert analysis into action: reallocate operating and capital expenditure,
set growth and margin targets per unit, define M&A or partnership moves, and
codify exit or harvest plans
([ClickUp guide](https://clickup.com/blog/bcg-growth-share-matrix/)).

Cadence: refresh the matrix at least annually as part of strategic planning,
more often in volatile sectors
([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/strategy-frameworks/bcg-growth-share-matrix/)),
and pair the annual review with quarterly operational reviews so cash can be
reallocated when Stars underperform or Question Marks break through
([Mooncamp](https://mooncamp.com/glossary/bcg-growth-share-matrix)). BCG's 2014
guidance stresses accelerating the cycle: in fast-moving markets, units should
move between quadrants faster, and the portfolio should hold more Question Marks
run as strategic experiments
([BCG Classics Revisited](https://bcghendersoninstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/BCG-Classics-Revisited-The-Growth-Share-Matrix.pdf)).

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **[Ansoff Matrix](./ansoff-matrix.md)** (1957) is the natural companion: it
  predates the BCG matrix and answers _how to grow_ a given unit (penetration,
  market development, product development, diversification), while the
  Growth–Share Matrix answers _where to invest_ across the portfolio. Ansoff
  identifies growth paths; BCG allocates capital
  ([Ansoff matrix — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansoff_matrix)).
- **GE–McKinsey Matrix** (early 1970s) is the direct rival: a 3×3 grid scoring
  industry attractiveness and business strength on multiple weighted criteria
  instead of BCG's two proxies. Practitioners use BCG for rapid screening and
  GE–McKinsey for refined analysis
  ([CaseBasix comparison](https://www.casebasix.com/pages/bcg-matrix-vs-ge-mckinsey-matrix)).
- **McKinsey Three Horizons Model** (1999) adds a time dimension the BCG matrix
  lacks, structuring the portfolio into core business, emerging businesses, and
  early-stage options
  ([Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/strategy-frameworks/mckinsey-three-horizons-of-growth/)).
- **Experience Curve** (BCG, 1966–1968) is the predecessor concept the matrix is
  built on: unit costs fall 20–30% per doubling of accumulated volume, so share
  leadership confers cost advantage
  ([BCG Classics Revisited: The Experience Curve](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2013/growth-business-unit-strategy-experience-curve-bcg-classics-revisited)).
- **[SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md)** works one level down: after the matrix
  assigns a quadrant, SWOT examines a single unit's position to shape its
  strategy.
- **[Impact / Effort](./impact-effort.md)** applies the same
  prioritize-by-two-proxies logic at task and project scale rather than
  business-unit scale.

## What is the core insight?

A multi-product company should manage its businesses as a cash-flow portfolio —
milking mature leaders to fund tomorrow's leaders — rather than letting each
unit reinvest its own earnings.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. Neither "BCG Matrix" nor "Growth-Share Matrix" appears among Boston
Consulting Group's registered trademarks in the USPTO database
([Justia trademark listing](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/the-boston-consulting-group-inc-1111517/page3)),
and the framework is taught and applied without licensing. BCG's published texts
— Henderson's 1970 essay and the retrospectives — remain under copyright and may
be cited but not republished
([BCG Terms of Use](https://www.bcg.com/about/terms-of-use)). The underlying
concepts (portfolio segmentation by growth and market position, cash-flow logic)
are public domain; attribution to BCG and Henderson is scholarly best practice.
