# The OK Corral

The OK Corral is a 2×2 grid of "life positions" from Transactional Analysis
(TA). The horizontal axis is your view of yourself — **I'm OK / I'm not OK** —
and the vertical axis is your view of the other person — **You're OK / You're
not OK**. The four resulting quadrants are each a stance toward the world and an
operation that follows from it: **I'm OK, You're OK** (**get-on-with** — the
healthy, collaborative position), **I'm OK, You're not OK** (**get-rid-of** —
angry, superior, blaming), **I'm not OK, You're OK** (**get-away-from** —
helpless, inferior, withdrawing), and **I'm not OK, You're not OK**
(**get-nowhere-with** — hopeless, stuck). It is used in therapy, coaching, and
management training to name the position a person (or a conversation) is
operating from and to steer deliberately back toward I'm-OK-You're-OK
([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409),
[lifewithkindness.co.uk](https://lifewithkindness.co.uk/okcorralorigins)).

## When was it created?

In three steps across the 1960s and early 1970s. Eric Berne developed
Transactional Analysis in the late 1950s
([Wikipedia: Transactional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_analysis))
and first delineated the four life positions in his 1962 paper "Classification
of Positions" in the Transactional Analysis Bulletin (discussed in
[White, 1994](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/036215379402400406)
and
[Jacobs, 1997](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215379702700309)).
Thomas A. Harris popularized the positions for a mass audience with the 1967
book _I'm OK – You're OK_, a huge bestseller after 1972
([Wikipedia: I'm OK – You're OK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK)).
The grid itself — the OK Corral diagram with its four "get" operations — was
drawn by Franklin H. Ernst Jr. and published in 1971 as "The OK Corral: The Grid
for Get-on-With," Transactional Analysis Journal, 1(4), 33–42
([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409)).

## Who created it?

Three people share the provenance.
[Eric Berne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Berne), the psychiatrist who
founded TA, originated the underlying concept of life positions (Berne, 1962,
"Classification of Positions," Transactional Analysis Bulletin; see
[Jacobs, 1997](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215379702700309)).
[Thomas A. Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK),
another psychiatrist and Berne colleague, gave the positions their famous
phrasing and reach through _I'm OK – You're OK_ (1967). Franklin H. Ernst Jr.,
an early TA contributor, arranged the positions into the 2×2 grid, named it the
"OK Corral" (a pun on the Tombstone gunfight site), and attached the four
behavioral operations — get-on-with, get-rid-of, get-away-from, get-nowhere-with
([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409);
Ernst's own later monograph,
[_Transactional Analysis in the OK Corral: Grid for What's Happening_](http://ernstokcorral.com/Publications/OK%20CORRAL%20monograph.pdf)).
Note that no standalone Wikipedia article exists for the OK Corral grid or for
life positions as such; the concepts are covered inside the
[Transactional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_analysis)
and
[I'm OK – You're OK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK)
articles.

The creators also disagreed. Berne held that positions are decided in early
childhood and that the child's natural starting state is I'm OK, You're OK;
Harris argued instead that I'm not OK, You're OK is the universal position every
child adopts (seeing adults as capable and themselves as small), and that
reaching I'm OK, You're OK is a conscious adult decision, often requiring
therapy
([Wikipedia: I'm OK – You're OK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK)).
Ernst added a third reading: the quadrants are not only fixed life scripts but
"dynamic social operations" people move between moment to moment in ordinary
encounters
([lifewithkindness.co.uk](https://lifewithkindness.co.uk/okcorralorigins)).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Berne wanted a plain-language alternative to psychoanalysis — a way for patients
themselves to see the stances driving their transactions and games
([Wikipedia: Transactional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_analysis)).
Harris's problem was reach: translating TA into a self-help vocabulary that
ordinary readers could apply to their own "not OK" feelings without a
therapist's mediation
([Wikipedia: I'm OK – You're OK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK)).
Ernst's problem was diagnostic: the four positions existed as prose, but
practitioners lacked a visual instrument for spotting, in a live encounter,
which position a person was operating from and what social outcome it was
producing. The grid solved that by pairing each quadrant with an observable
resolution — getting on with someone, getting rid of them, getting away from
them, or getting nowhere with them
([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It compresses a whole interpersonal dynamic into two questions anyone can ask
  mid-conversation: _am I treating myself as OK?_ and _am I treating you as OK?_
- Each quadrant predicts a behavior, not just a feeling: superiority slides
  toward get-rid-of (blame, criticism), inferiority toward get-away-from
  (withdrawal, appeasement), hopelessness toward get-nowhere-with — so naming
  the quadrant suggests the corrective move.
- Only one quadrant — I'm OK, You're OK — produces get-on-with outcomes, which
  gives the model a clear, non-punitive target state
  ([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409)).
- In Ernst's dynamic reading, everyone visits all four corners; the grid is a
  noticing tool rather than a personality verdict
  ([lifewithkindness.co.uk](https://lifewithkindness.co.uk/okcorralorigins)).
- It has a half-century track record in TA psychotherapy, counselling training,
  and management education
  ([Wikipedia: Transactional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_analysis)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used honestly, the grid makes a person's default position visible — the corner
they retreat to under stress — and makes the moment of sliding out of
I'm-OK-You're-OK catchable while it is still a choice. Ernst titled the paper
"the grid for get-on-with" because that is the point: the other three corners
are mapped so that encounters can be deliberately steered back to the one where
both parties stay OK and things get resolved
([Ernst, 1971](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/036215377100100409)).
Harris's larger claim — that adults can consciously re-decide their childhood
position and live from I'm OK, You're OK — is the book's promise rather than a
measured result, and Berne himself disputed Harris's starting premise, so the
honest expectation is the smaller one: a fast, memorable diagnostic for where
you and the other person stand right now, and a nudge toward the get-on-with
corner
([Wikipedia: I'm OK – You're OK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27m_OK_%E2%80%93_You%27re_OK)).
