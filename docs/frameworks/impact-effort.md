# Impact/Effort Matrix

The Impact/Effort Matrix (also called the Action Priority Matrix or
Impact/Effort Chart) is a 2×2 quadrant model for prioritizing candidate work
along two axes: **impact** (the value delivered if done) and **effort** (the
cost of doing it). The four quadrants are: high impact / low effort — **Quick
Wins**; high impact / high effort — **Big Bets** (often called Major Projects);
low impact / low effort — **Fill-Ins**; and low impact / high effort —
**Thankless Tasks** (also called Time Wasters or Money Pits). The prescription
is to do Quick Wins first, plan Big Bets deliberately, use Fill-Ins for spare
moments, and avoid Thankless Tasks.

## When was it created?

Unknown. Unlike the [Eisenhower Matrix](./eisenhower-matrix.md) or
[Bezos's doors](./one-way-two-way-doors.md), no dated origin for the
impact-versus-effort 2×2 has surfaced in the sources consulted for this
document, and vendor articles that describe the tool consistently decline to
name a date. The most that can be said honestly: payoff-versus-difficulty grids
were in circulation in the quality-improvement world by the late twentieth
century — the closely related
[PICK chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PICK_chart) came out of Lockheed
Martin's Lean/Six Sigma practice (itself undated on Wikipedia) — and the "Action
Priority Matrix" packaging with the Quick Wins / Major Projects / Fill-Ins /
Thankless Tasks labels was popularized online by
[MindTools](https://www.mindtools.com/agst6d0/the-action-priority-matrix/) in
the 2000s. Treat any source that states a precise origin date with suspicion.

## Who created it?

No credible individual creator is identified anywhere in the literature
consulted; this is a folk tool. Three honest observations about attribution:

- The nearest documented relative with a named institutional origin is the
  [PICK chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PICK_chart) (Possible / Implement /
  Challenge / Kill), which Wikipedia states "was initially developed by Lockheed
  Martin" for prioritizing process-improvement ideas on
  payoff-versus-ease-of-implementation axes — structurally the same grid with
  different labels.
- Lean Six Sigma glossaries (e.g.
  [Six Sigma DSI](https://sixsigmadsi.com/glossary/impact-effort-chart/)) treat
  the impact/effort chart as a standard continuous-improvement tool without
  attributing it, and
  [practitioner articles](https://safetyculture.com/topics/impact-effort-matrix)
  describe it as inspired by earlier 2×2 prioritization grids such as the
  Eisenhower Matrix.
- The specific "Action Priority Matrix" name and quadrant labels are associated
  with
  [MindTools'](https://www.mindtools.com/agst6d0/the-action-priority-matrix/)
  training material, which popularized rather than invented the underlying idea.

There is no Wikipedia article for the Impact/Effort Matrix itself as of this
writing; the PICK chart article is the closest covered relative.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

The recurring problem in the communities where the grid appears (quality
improvement, product management, team facilitation) is a surplus of candidate
ideas and a scarcity of capacity: after a brainstorm or in a backlog, everything
looks worth doing. Ranking by benefit alone ignores cost; ranking by ease alone
produces trivia. The matrix was adopted to force a joint judgment of value and
cost so a group can converge quickly on which few items deserve the next unit of
effort ([PICK chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PICK_chart);
[Six Sigma DSI](https://sixsigmadsi.com/glossary/impact-effort-chart/)).

## Why should someone use it?

It is one of the fastest defensible prioritization methods that exists: two
rough judgments per item, no scoring formulas, and the output is immediately
actionable because each quadrant carries a default disposition. It works well as
a group exercise — placing items on a shared grid surfaces disagreement about
impact or effort estimates that a flat ranked list hides. And it specifically
inoculates against the two most common prioritization failures: chasing
high-effort/low-impact "thankless tasks" out of momentum, and never starting
high-impact "big bets" because quick wins are more comfortable.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the team ships the Quick Wins early (visible momentum at low
cost), commits deliberate, scheduled capacity to a small number of Big Bets
(where most long-term value lives), keeps Fill-Ins as an opportunistic buffer,
and consciously declines Thankless Tasks. The expected net effect is a higher
ratio of delivered value to effort spent and an explicit, shared record of what
was chosen _not_ to do. A known caveat: both axes are estimates, and effort in
particular is habitually underestimated — the matrix's output is only as good as
the honesty of the placements.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

Thin. No dedicated peer-reviewed study validating the Impact/Effort Matrix as a
standalone tool appears in the literature consulted; what exists is adjacent
research, practitioner claims, and one well-documented critique:

- **Adjacent research.** The
  [National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools](https://www.nccmt.ca/organizational-change/results/2)
  lists the matrix in its evidence-informed organizational-change toolkit, and
  behavioral-economics work on planning fallacy and optimism bias explains why a
  tool forcing explicit value/cost judgments should help
  ([monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/project-management/impact-effort-matrix/)).
- **Practitioner claims.** Vendor guides report efficiency gains of up to 25%
  for teams using structured prioritization frameworks
  ([monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/project-management/impact-effort-matrix/)),
  but this figure is not independently verified for this specific tool.
- **Evidence against naive use.**
  [Itamar Gilad](https://itamargilad.com/why-impact-effort-prioritization-doesnt-work/)
  documents that both axes are systematically mis-estimated: teams underestimate
  effort by 2–3× (planning fallacy), and experiment data from Microsoft,
  Netflix, and Booking.com shows at best 1 in 3 ideas tested produced any
  measurable positive result, with value overestimated by up to a factor of 9.
  The implication is that unvalidated placements drift many "Quick Wins" and
  "Big Bets" into the Money Pit quadrant in hindsight.

The honest summary: the matrix's ordering logic (value up, cost down) is
uncontroversial, its group-alignment benefit is widely reported, and its
predictive accuracy is only as good as the estimates fed into it — for which the
best available data is unflattering.

## How does one use it properly?

The canonical workflow, consistent across practitioner guides
([The Digital Project Manager](https://thedigitalprojectmanager.com/productivity/impact-effort-matrix/);
[Six Sigma DSI](https://sixsigmadsi.com/what-is-an-impact-effort-matrix-how-does-it-work/);
[Product School](https://productschool.com/blog/product-fundamentals/impact-effort-matrix)):

1. **Set up the grid.** Draw a 2×2 on a whiteboard or digital canvas: impact on
   the vertical axis (low to high), effort on the horizontal (low to high).
2. **Define the axes for your context.** Agree what impact means (revenue,
   adoption, risk reduction) and what effort means (hours, people, complexity)
   before placing anything.
3. **Score each item.** Assign rough impact and effort ratings (a 1–10 scale
   suffices) per candidate item.
4. **Place items as a group.** Map items to quadrants by consensus; the
   disagreements surfaced during placement are part of the value.
5. **Apply the quadrant dispositions.** Quick Wins first, Big Bets scheduled
   with deliberate capacity, Fill-Ins held as buffer, Thankless Tasks declined.
6. **Sequence within quadrants.** Order by priority and dependencies.
7. **Assign ownership.** Give each accepted item an owner and a timeline.
8. **Review on a cadence.** Rerun during backlog refinement (quarterly is a
   common cadence per
   [monday.com](https://monday.com/blog/project-management/impact-effort-matrix/))
   and whenever strategy, capacity, or external conditions shift.

To harden the estimates,
[Gilad](https://itamargilad.com/why-impact-effort-prioritization-doesnt-work/)
recommends: break impact and effort into subcomponents; compare against data
from similar past projects; run cheap validation (surveys, smoke tests,
interviews, MVPs) before committing to Big Bets; add a confidence multiplier
(Prio = Impact ÷ Effort × Confidence); and A/B test where possible.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **[Eisenhower Matrix](./eisenhower-matrix.md)** — the conceptual predecessor
  among prioritization 2×2s, crossing urgency with importance instead of value
  with cost ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenhower_matrix)). Use
  Eisenhower for personal task triage, Impact/Effort for project and feature
  selection.
- **[PICK chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PICK_chart)** — structurally
  identical sibling from Lockheed Martin's Lean Six Sigma practice (Possible /
  Implement / Challenge / Kill on payoff-versus-ease axes).
- **[How–Now–Wow](./how-now-wow.md)** — a neighboring 2×2 for sorting brainstorm
  output by originality and feasibility; it selects ideas, after which
  Impact/Effort sequences them.
- **[Importance × Satisfaction](./importance-satisfaction.md)** — locates
  opportunity (important but unsatisfied needs); its output feeds the impact
  axis of this matrix.
- **RICE** (Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort) — a numeric refinement that
  splits reach from impact and adds explicit confidence; more precise and
  data-backed but slower to run
  ([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/guides/rice-score-prioritization-framework-product-management);
  [PM Toolkit comparison](https://pmtoolkit.ai/learn/prioritization/prioritization-frameworks-comparison)).
  Comparisons recommend Impact/Effort or ICE for 30-minute whiteboard decisions,
  RICE or weighted scoring when stakeholder buy-in needs numbers.
- **ICE** (Impact, Confidence, Ease) —
  [Gilad's recommended refinement](https://itamargilad.com/tag/ice-scores/):
  keeps the matrix's speed while patching its blind spot on estimate uncertainty
  via an explicit confidence score.
- **MoSCoW** (Must / Should / Could / Won't-have) — categorical requirements
  prioritization from 1990s software practice; classifies instead of plotting
  ([Guru](https://www.getguru.com/reference/moscow-methodology)).
- **[Kano Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kano_model)** (Noriaki
  Kano, 1984) — classifies features by customer-satisfaction type. A common
  two-pass workflow uses Kano to decide what is worth building, then
  Impact/Effort or RICE to sequence it
  ([Plane](https://plane.so/blog/feature-prioritization-frameworks-rice-moscow-and-kano-explained)).

## What is the core insight?

Value and cost are independent judgments, and only work that is explicitly
weighed on both at once — rather than ranked by benefit or ease alone — reveals
which few items deserve the next unit of effort.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. The impact-versus-effort 2×2 is an unattributed, unbranded generic
concept with no trademark or patent claims found in the sources consulted; Lean
Six Sigma bodies treat it as a public-domain standard tool
([Six Sigma DSI](https://sixsigmadsi.com/glossary/impact-effort-chart/)).
[MindTools](https://www.mindtools.com/agst6d0/the-action-priority-matrix/)
popularized the "Action Priority Matrix" packaging but asserts no trademark on
it, and the term is used generically across the industry. As with any framework,
a specific published diagram or template can carry copyright as a creative work
— some templates are distributed under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike
4.0
([Facilitator School](https://www.facilitator.school/template/impact-effort-matrix))
— but the underlying grid concept is not protectable, so drawing your own matrix
requires no license.
