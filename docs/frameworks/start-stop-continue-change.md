# Start / Stop / Continue / Change

Start / Stop / Continue is a feedback and retrospective format built on three
plain-language questions: what should we **Start** doing (new practices worth
adopting), what should we **Stop** doing (practices that are not paying off),
and what should we **Continue** doing (practices that are working and should be
protected)? This app's template adds a fourth quadrant, **Change** — practices
worth keeping in some form but not as-is — turning the list into a 2×2 of
existing-versus-new practices against embrace-versus-rethink. A session runs as
a structured brainstorm: each participant silently writes items for each
category, the group clusters and discusses them, and the team commits to a small
number of concrete actions before the next cycle. In agile teams it is one of
the most common sprint-retrospective formats
([SKS process — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_process)); outside
agile it survives as a performance-feedback and survey device in management and
coaching practice ([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)).

## When was it created?

No documented creation date exists — this is a folk format with roots in
management and organizational-development feedback practice that predate agile.
The earliest concrete, attributable appearance in print is Harvard Business
School professor Thomas J. DeLong's account of the "SKS form" (Stop / Keep /
Start), which he says he learned in graduate school from psychology professor
Phil Daniels at Brigham Young University — placing the technique's classroom use
decades before DeLong wrote about it in
[Harvard Business Review in August 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback).
As a team retrospective activity, it entered the agile mainstream after Norm
Kerth's _Project Retrospectives_ (2001) established the retrospective ritual and
Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's
[_Agile Retrospectives: Making Good Teams Great_](https://pragprog.com/titles/dlret/agile-retrospectives/)
(Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2006) catalogued decide-what-to-do activities of this
shape; [Retromat](https://retromat.org/) likewise lists the closely related
"Start, Stop, Continue, More of, Less of" wheel from that lineage. The
four-quadrant **Change** variant has no identifiable published origin; it is one
of several modern extensions (alongside "More of / Less of") layered onto the
classic trio.

## Who created it?

Unknown — and honesty requires saying so. The strongest attribution thread
credits
[Phil Daniels](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback),
a Brigham Young University psychology professor, with teaching the Stop / Keep /
Start ("SKS") question set, as reported by Thomas J. DeLong, who later spread it
through Harvard Business School and Wall Street performance reviews. But Daniels
is documented as a teacher of the technique, not necessarily its inventor, and
near-identical stop/start/continue framings appear independently across HR,
coaching, and education literature
([Wikipedia's SKS process article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_process)
describes the agile Start/Stop/Continue as the same approach under another
name). No one person can honestly be credited with the format; Derby and
Larsen's 2006 book and the agile community popularized it as a retrospective
activity without claiming to have invented it.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Unstructured feedback tends to be vague ("do better"), skewed negative, or
skewed polite. The SKS/Start-Stop-Continue questions force feedback to be
**behavioral and actionable**: every item names a specific practice and an
explicit disposition toward it — begin it, end it, or keep it
([DeLong, HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback)).
The "Continue" category also solves a subtler problem: reviews and retros that
only list faults destroy morale and accidentally discard what was working.
Within agile, the format answers the retrospective's core need — turning an
iteration's raw experience into a small set of process changes — with a
structure simple enough that a team can run it with no trained facilitator
([Retrospective — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrospective)). The
Change quadrant addresses the false binary between Stop and Continue: some
practices are worth keeping only if modified.

## Why should someone use it?

Because it is the lowest-ceremony structured retro that still produces
decisions. The three (or four) questions require no explanation, work for teams
and for individual feedback alike, and force balance — positives are solicited
on equal footing with negatives. Compared to open-ended discussion, the
categories convert complaints into dispositions: an item cannot land on the
board without an implied action attached. It is a good default for teams new to
retrospectives, for quick end-of-project reviews, and for feedback cycles
(engagement surveys, leadership 360s) outside software entirely
([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)). The Change variant
suits teams who find themselves arguing "stop it — no, keep it" about practices
that really just need adjusting.

## What is the expected outcome?

A short, agreed list of concrete behavior changes: a handful of practices the
team commits to begin, end, modify, or protect in the next iteration — with the
sorting itself surfacing consensus and disagreement about what is actually
working. Used as intended (recurring, with items revisited the following cycle),
it produces incremental continuous improvement of the team's process and, in its
feedback-form guise, specific behavioral guidance a person can act on
immediately rather than a numeric rating
([DeLong, HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback)).
The failure mode it guards against — retros that vent but change nothing — is
avoided precisely because every category is an imperative verb.
