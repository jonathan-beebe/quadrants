# Start / Stop / Continue / Change

> **Protect vs. rethink:** every practice on the board gets an imperative —
> start, stop, continue, or adjust — so feedback ends in commitments, not
> complaints.

Start / Stop / Continue is a feedback and retrospective format built on three
plain-language questions: what should we **Start** doing (new practices worth
adopting), what should we **Stop** doing (practices that are not paying off),
and what should we **Continue** doing (practices that are working and should be
protected)? This app's template adds a fourth quadrant, **Change** — practices
worth keeping in some form but not as-is — turning the list into a 2×2 with
**existing versus new** practices on one axis and **embrace versus rethink** on
the other. A session runs as a structured brainstorm: each participant silently
writes items for each category, the group clusters and discusses them, and the
team commits to a small number of concrete actions before the next cycle. In
agile teams it is one of the most common sprint-retrospective formats
([SKS process — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_process)); outside
agile it survives as a performance-feedback and survey device in management and
coaching practice ([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)).

## When was it created?

No documented creation date exists — this is a folk format with roots in
management and organizational-development feedback practice that predate agile.
The earliest concrete, attributable appearance in print is Harvard Business
School professor Thomas J. DeLong's account of the "SKS form" (Stop / Keep /
Start), which he says he learned in graduate school from psychology professor
Phil Daniels at Brigham Young University — placing the technique's classroom use
decades before DeLong wrote about it in
[Harvard Business Review in August 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback).
As a team retrospective activity, it entered the agile mainstream after Norm
Kerth's _Project Retrospectives_ (2001) established the retrospective ritual and
Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's
[_Agile Retrospectives: Making Good Teams Great_](https://pragprog.com/titles/dlret/agile-retrospectives/)
(Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2006) catalogued decide-what-to-do activities of this
shape; [Retromat](https://retromat.org/) likewise lists the closely related
"Start, Stop, Continue, More of, Less of" wheel from that lineage. The
four-quadrant **Change** variant has no identifiable published origin; it is one
of several modern extensions (alongside "More of / Less of") layered onto the
classic trio.

## Who created it?

Unknown — and honesty requires saying so. The strongest attribution thread
credits
[Phil Daniels](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback),
a Brigham Young University psychology professor, with teaching the Stop / Keep /
Start ("SKS") question set, as reported by Thomas J. DeLong, who later spread it
through Harvard Business School and Wall Street performance reviews. But Daniels
is documented as a teacher of the technique, not necessarily its inventor, and
near-identical stop/start/continue framings appear independently across HR,
coaching, and education literature
([Wikipedia's SKS process article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_process)
describes the agile Start/Stop/Continue as the same approach under another
name). No one person can honestly be credited with the format; Derby and
Larsen's 2006 book and the agile community popularized it as a retrospective
activity without claiming to have invented it.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Unstructured feedback tends to be vague ("do better"), skewed negative, or
skewed polite. The SKS/Start-Stop-Continue questions force feedback to be
**behavioral and actionable**: every item names a specific practice and an
explicit disposition toward it — begin it, end it, or keep it
([DeLong, HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback)).
The "Continue" category also solves a subtler problem: reviews and retros that
only list faults destroy morale and accidentally discard what was working.
Within agile, the format answers the retrospective's core need — turning an
iteration's raw experience into a small set of process changes — with a
structure simple enough that a team can run it with no trained facilitator
([Retrospective — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrospective)). The
Change quadrant addresses the false binary between Stop and Continue: some
practices are worth keeping only if modified.

## Why should someone use it?

Because it is the lowest-ceremony structured retro that still produces
decisions. The three (or four) questions require no explanation, work for teams
and for individual feedback alike, and force balance — positives are solicited
on equal footing with negatives. Compared to open-ended discussion, the
categories convert complaints into dispositions: an item cannot land on the
board without an implied action attached. It is a good default for teams new to
retrospectives, for quick end-of-project reviews, and for feedback cycles
(engagement surveys, leadership 360s) outside software entirely
([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)). The Change variant
suits teams who find themselves arguing "stop it — no, keep it" about practices
that really just need adjusting.

## What is the expected outcome?

A short, agreed list of concrete behavior changes: a handful of practices the
team commits to begin, end, modify, or protect in the next iteration — with the
sorting itself surfacing consensus and disagreement about what is actually
working. Used as intended (recurring, with items revisited the following cycle),
it produces incremental continuous improvement of the team's process and, in its
feedback-form guise, specific behavioral guidance a person can act on
immediately rather than a numeric rating
([DeLong, HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback)).
The failure mode it guards against — retros that vent but change nothing — is
avoided precisely because every category is an imperative verb.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

There is none in the empirical sense: no peer-reviewed studies, controlled
trials, or longitudinal data validating Start/Stop/Continue (or any variant)
were located. The primary written source,
[DeLong's HBR article](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback),
is a practitioner essay presenting no research data; the
[Wikipedia SKS process article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKS_process) cites
practical applications but no effectiveness metrics; and
[Derby and Larsen's book](https://pragprog.com/titles/dlret/agile-retrospectives/)
is a recipe catalogue, not a study. Claimed benefits — open communication,
continuous improvement, engagement, actionable insight — appear in vendor and
practitioner sources without supporting data
([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)). No published
critiques, failure analyses, or comparative-effectiveness studies of
retrospective formats were found either; the case for the framework rests on
decades of practitioner adoption, not measurement.

## How do you get into the right mindset?

The literature documents preparation rather than priming: name the session's
purpose and limit it to a single topic or iteration
([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)), and establish safety
before anyone writes — open, blameless sharing, with anonymous submission or the
Chatham House Rule when candor is at risk
([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)). No
source offers warm-up questions beyond that, so the orienting questions below
are derived from the format's own categories and core insight:

- What did we do this cycle — not what we planned, but the habits we ran?
- Which of those practices would I defend if someone tried to take them away?
- What am I tolerating that I would not choose again?
- Is my complaint with a practice itself, or with how we run it? The first is a
  Stop; the second is a Change.

Answers should stay behavioral — name practices, not people.

## How does one use it properly?

Run it as a recurring, time-boxed session — end of sprint or project cycle in
agile, typically an hour or two; per performance cycle in feedback settings
([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue)). A structured pass
looks like this ([Tempo](https://www.tempo.io/blog/start-stop-continue);
[Atlassian Team Playbook](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)):

1. **Prepare** — set the objective and scope; a single topic or iteration keeps
   the board manageable.
2. **Set the tone** (~5 min) — establish safety; use anonymous submission tools
   when candor is at risk.
3. **Gather input** (~15 min) — each participant silently writes items into
   Start, Stop, Continue, and Change.
4. **Cluster and discuss** (~20 min) — group duplicates, identify themes, turn
   feedback into insights.
5. **Prioritize and assign** (~15 min) — dot-vote to converge, pick a small
   number of actions, and give each an owner
   ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)).
6. **Track** — document the actions and revisit them at the start of the next
   session.

Keep granularity behavioral: each item names one specific, observable practice.
In its feedback-form guise, DeLong recommends capping each category at three
bullet points so the recipient gets focus rather than a laundry list
([DeLong, HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback)).
Sort borderline items by the axis: keep as-is goes to Continue, keep-but-modify
goes to Change, so Stop stays reserved for practices genuinely worth ending.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

It is one activity within the broader retrospective ritual Norm Kerth formalized
in 2001, alongside kin such as after-action reviews, debriefs, and
lessons-learned exercises
([Retrospective — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrospective)). Its
closest siblings in this library are the other retrospective 2×2s:
[Keep / Problem / Try / Question](./keep-problem-try-question.md), which adds a
stateful loop (Try items get re-sorted next session), and
[Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave](./love-loathe-learn-leave.md), which sorts by
feeling rather than disposition — this library's analog of the "4 Ls" (Loved /
Loathed / Learned / Longed for) and Sad / Mad / Glad emotional formats
([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)).
Direct same-lineage variants include the "Start, Stop, Continue, More of, Less
of" wheel ([Retromat](https://retromat.org/)) and futurespective forms such as
Hopes & Concerns and Now / Next / Later
([Fun Retrospectives](https://www.funretrospectives.com/)). It pairs well with
prioritization frameworks downstream of the session:
[Impact / Effort](./impact-effort.md) or [How–Now–Wow](./how-now-wow.md) can
rank the Start and Change items a retro produces, and
[Importance × Satisfaction](./importance-satisfaction.md) offers the same
keep/fix/drop logic applied to features rather than practices.
[SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md) is the strategic-planning cousin: both force
balanced positive/negative inventories, but SWOT assesses position while
Start/Stop/Continue/Change assigns dispositions to behaviors.

## What is the core insight?

Feedback becomes actionable when every observation must be filed under an
imperative verb — start, stop, continue, or change — so nothing can be said
without a disposition attached.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. The framework is a folk practice in the public domain: no trademark
exists on "Start Stop Continue" or its variants, the question set is not a
copyrighted instrument, and no licensing or permission is required. Specific
texts about it are copyrighted — DeLong's
[HBR article](https://hbr.org/2011/08/three-questions-for-effective-feedback) by
HBR, and
[Derby and Larsen's book](https://pragprog.com/titles/dlret/agile-retrospectives/)
by Pragmatic Bookshelf — but the underlying activity predates and exists
independently of both. The structure is freely adaptable under any name: Stop /
Keep / Start (the original SKS form), Begin / End / Maintain, or the
four-quadrant Start / Stop / Continue / Change used here.
