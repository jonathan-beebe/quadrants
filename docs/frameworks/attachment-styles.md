# Attachment Styles

The two-dimensional model of adult attachment is a 2×2 framework from
relationship psychology. The two axes are **attachment anxiety** (fear of
abandonment and preoccupation with a partner's availability) and **attachment
avoidance** (discomfort with closeness and reluctance to depend on others).
Crossing them yields four styles: **secure** (low anxiety, low avoidance),
**preoccupied** — also called anxious-preoccupied — (high anxiety, low
avoidance), **dismissive-avoidant** (low anxiety, high avoidance), and
**fearful-avoidant** (high on both). In the original formulation the axes were
framed as one's internal working **model of self** (positive or negative) and
**model of other** (positive or negative)
([Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attachment-styles-among-young-adults:-a-test-of-a-Bartholomew-Horowitz/6b6000ae9911fa9f9ec6345048b5a20501bdcedf));
later measurement work showed those map closely onto the anxiety and avoidance
dimensions
([Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/brennan.html)).
The framework is used in research via questionnaires such as the ECR, and
popularly as a self-reflection tool for understanding recurring patterns in
close relationships.

## When was it created?

In stages spanning four decades. The roots are John Bowlby's attachment theory,
developed from the late 1950s through his _Attachment and Loss_ trilogy
(1969–1980), and Mary Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" studies of the 1970s,
which classified infants as secure, avoidant, or anxious/ambivalent
([Wikipedia: Attachment theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_theory)).
In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver extended those three infant categories to
adult romantic love
([Hazan & Shaver, 1987, _JPSP_, 52(3), 511–524](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3572722/)).
The four-quadrant model itself dates to 1991, when Kim Bartholomew and Leonard
Horowitz published "Attachment Styles Among Young Adults: A Test of a
Four-Category Model," defining secure, preoccupied, dismissing, and fearful
styles from positive/negative models of self and other
([Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991, _JPSP_, 61(2), 226–244](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1341430)).
The dimensional axes were cemented in 1998, when Brennan, Clark, and Shaver
factor-analyzed the existing self-report measures, found they reduced to two
orthogonal dimensions — anxiety and avoidance — and published the Experiences in
Close Relationships (ECR) questionnaire
([Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1617488)).

## Who created it?

No single person. [John Bowlby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bowlby)
created attachment theory itself, and
[Mary Ainsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Ainsworth) supplied its
empirical method and the original three-category typology of infants. Cindy
Hazan and Phillip Shaver were the first to treat adult romantic love as an
attachment process, carrying the three styles into adulthood
([Hazan & Shaver, 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3572722/)). The 2×2
four-category model belongs to Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz, who also
introduced the Relationship Questionnaire (RQ) to measure it
([Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Attachment-styles-among-young-adults:-a-test-of-a-Bartholomew-Horowitz/6b6000ae9911fa9f9ec6345048b5a20501bdcedf)).
Kelly Brennan, Catherine Clark, and Phillip Shaver established the modern
anxiety × avoidance dimensional structure and the ECR
([Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/brennan.html)).
The provenance is unusually well documented, though the field still carries a
split inherited from it: a categorical tradition (four types, following
Bartholomew) and a dimensional tradition (two continuous scores, following
Brennan et al.), and researchers today largely favor the dimensional view
([Domic-Siede et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359990/)).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Each layer addressed a different gap. Bowlby wanted to explain why early
separation from caregivers had such lasting effects on emotional development,
proposing that children form internal working models of self and others that
persist into later relationships
([Wikipedia: Attachment theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_theory)).
Hazan and Shaver noticed that adult romantic love had no comparable explanatory
framework — why do some adults trust easily while others cling or withdraw? —
and tested whether Ainsworth's infant categories translated to adult romance
(they did, with roughly 56% of adults self-describing as secure, 25% avoidant,
and 19% anxious)
([Hazan & Shaver, 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3572722/)). Bartholomew
and Horowitz were solving a specific puzzle in that scheme: "avoidance"
conflated two different people — those who dismiss closeness because they don't
need it (positive self-model) and those who fear it despite wanting it (negative
self-model). Splitting avoidance on the self-model axis produced the
four-quadrant grid
([Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1341430)).
Brennan, Clark, and Shaver were solving a measurement mess: dozens of competing
questionnaires existed, and their factor analysis showed all of them reduced to
the same two dimensions
([Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1617488)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It replaces a vague question ("why do my relationships keep going wrong?")
  with two specific, answerable ones: _how anxious am I about abandonment?_ and
  _how uncomfortable am I with closeness?_
- The fourfold split is genuinely diagnostic of different dynamics: anxious
  people use hyperactivating strategies (energetic bids for proximity and
  reassurance) while avoidant people use deactivating strategies (denying
  attachment needs, keeping distance) — so the useful response differs by
  quadrant
  ([Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3266769/)).
- It rests on one of the deepest evidence bases of any 2×2 framework: decades of
  developmental research plus a validated, widely used instrument (the ECR and
  its successors)
  ([Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/brennan.html)).
- It is compassion-friendly: styles are learned adaptations to attachment
  history, not character flaws, which makes the framework usable without blame.

One caution: the styles describe tendencies on continuous dimensions, not fixed
diagnoses. Most researchers now treat anxiety and avoidance as sliding scales
rather than boxes, and attachment patterns can and do change — studies suggest
roughly 20–30% of people shift styles over time
([Wikipedia: Attachment in adults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_in_adults),
[Domic-Siede et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359990/)).
Treat a quadrant label as a starting point for reflection, not an identity or a
clinical verdict.

## What is the expected outcome?

For an individual, the expected outcome is a more accurate account of one's own
relational reflexes: recognizing whether distress in a relationship tends to
surface as protest and reassurance-seeking (anxiety) or as withdrawal and
self-reliance (avoidance), and noticing the partner-side of the same patterns.
That awareness is the entry point to change, since insecure strategies are
workarounds that can be unlearned — attachment style is moderately stable but
not fixed
([Wikipedia: Attachment in adults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_in_adults)).
In research and clinical contexts, the dimensions predict meaningful outcomes:
attachment insecurity is linked to poorer emotion regulation (avoidant
individuals, for example, show impaired cognitive reappraisal under stress) and
to vulnerability to a range of psychological difficulties, while security
functions as a resilience resource
([Domic-Siede et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359990/),
[Mikulincer & Shaver, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3266769/)).
The honest expectation for everyday use is not a personality verdict but a map:
knowing which quadrant you drift toward under stress, and what secure
functioning would look like from there.
