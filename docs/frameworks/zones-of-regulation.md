# The Zones of Regulation

The Zones of Regulation is a self-regulation curriculum that sorts states of
arousal and emotional control into four color-coded zones: the **Blue Zone**
(low arousal — sad, tired, sick, bored), the **Green Zone** (calm, focused,
alert, ready to learn), the **Yellow Zone** (heightened arousal with some
control remaining — frustration, anxiety, excitement, silliness), and the **Red
Zone** (extreme arousal — anger, rage, panic, terror). Learners check in on
which zone they are in, learn their personal triggers, and build a toolbox of
strategies for moving between zones — with the explicit framing that "there are
no good zones or bad zones," only zones that do or don't match the current
situation
([CHADD Attention magazine, 2012](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf)).
One structural note for this collection: although it is often drawn as a
four-square grid, the Zones is not a true two-axis 2×2. It is a single
arousal/regulation continuum cut into four colored bands — Blue at the bottom,
Red at the top — closer in spirit to a thermometer or speedometer than to a
quadrant matrix like the Mood Meter, which crosses energy with pleasantness.

## When was it created?

The curriculum was published in 2011 as _The Zones of Regulation: A Curriculum
Designed to Foster Self-Regulation and Emotional Control_ (Social Thinking
Publishing, now Think Social Publishing), a lesson book with reproducible
visuals and handouts
([CHADD Attention magazine, 2012](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf),
[Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Zones_of_Regulation.html?id=ggKWZwEACAAJ)).
The framework itself predates the book slightly: Kuypers developed it as the
capstone of her graduate work in education, refining a visual tool she had been
using with her own students
([Beacon School Support podcast interview with Kuypers](https://beaconschoolsupport.co.uk/podcast/the-zones-of-regulation-how-to-help-kids-manage-strong-emotions-with-leah-kuypers)).
It has since grown into apps, storybooks, trainings, and a 2024 companion
volume, _Getting Into The Zones of Regulation_
([Think Social Publishing](https://www.socialthinking.com/zones-of-regulation)).

## Who created it?

[Leah Kuypers](https://zonesofregulation.com/meet-the-creator-and-founder/), an
occupational therapist (OTR/L) with a Master of Arts in Education and a graduate
certificate in autism from Hamline University in St. Paul, Minnesota. She
created the Zones while working in schools, out of frustration that students
overwhelmed by emotions were being punished rather than taught the missing
skills
([zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com/meet-the-creator-and-founder/),
[Beacon podcast](https://beaconschoolsupport.co.uk/podcast/the-zones-of-regulation-how-to-help-kids-manage-strong-emotions-with-leah-kuypers)).
The framework has clearly acknowledged ancestry in occupational therapy and
social cognition work: it draws on cognitive behavioral strategies and on
Michelle Garcia Winner's Social Thinking methodology (Winner's company published
the book)
([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf),
[Think Social Publishing](https://www.socialthinking.com/zones-of-regulation)),
on sensory-processing principles descending from A. Jean Ayres's sensory
integration tradition
([zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com/meet-the-creator-and-founder/)),
and on earlier OT self-regulation scales — most visibly the Alert Program's "How
Does Your Engine Run?" engine-speed metaphor (Williams & Shellenberger)
([alertprogram.com](https://alertprogram.com/)). Kuypers has also credited Kari
Dunn Buron, co-creator of the Incredible 5-Point Scale, who sat on her capstone
committee, and Ross Greene's "kids do well if they can" philosophy
([Beacon podcast](https://beaconschoolsupport.co.uk/podcast/the-zones-of-regulation-how-to-help-kids-manage-strong-emotions-with-leah-kuypers)).
No Wikipedia article exists for either the Zones of Regulation or Leah Kuypers;
the framework appears only in passing citations within other articles, so
primary and practitioner sources are cited here instead.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Most children develop self-regulation gradually and implicitly; some — notably
many students with autism, ADHD, or sensory-processing differences — do not, and
schools historically responded to the resulting behavior with discipline rather
than instruction. Kuypers's premise is that regulation is a teachable skill:
children who cannot yet answer "how do you feel?" can learn a simple shared
vocabulary (the four colors), learn to spot their triggers, and practice
concrete tools for shifting state, instead of being punished for lacking skills
nobody had taught them
([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf),
[zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com/meet-the-creator-and-founder/)).
The original curriculum packages this as eighteen lessons for K–12 learners,
taught in groups or individually
([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf)).

## Why should someone use it?

- The four colors give non-readers, young children, and learners with limited
  emotional vocabulary an immediately usable way to report internal states —
  Kuypers explains the zones with traffic-sign simplicity: red means stop,
  yellow means slow down, green means good to go, blue is a rest area
  ([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf)).
- It is deliberately non-judgmental: every zone is a normal human state, and the
  skill being taught is matching your zone to the situation, not "staying green"
  ([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf)).
- It pairs recognition with action — each zone maps to a personal toolbox of
  calming, alerting, or thinking strategies, so a check-in leads somewhere.
- It is very widely adopted, so a shared language likely already exists across a
  school or clinic; the publisher reports use in all 50 US states and more than
  40 countries ([zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com/)).
- Honest caveat: adoption has outrun evidence. An
  [ASAT review](https://asatonline.org/for-parents/becoming-a-savvy-consumer/zones-of-regulation-is-there-science-behind-that/)
  found little empirical support for the program with autistic learners, and a
  2024 systematic review (Mason et al.) concluded it does not yet meet the bar
  for an evidence-based practice. Its research base is far thinner than, for
  example, RULER's (see the Mood Meter doc). Choose it as a plausible, low-cost
  shared vocabulary — not as a validated intervention.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as designed — lessons on identifying zones, mapping triggers, and building
and rehearsing a personal toolbox — the intended outcome is a learner who can
notice their own state, name it in shared language, and deliberately apply a
strategy to reach the zone the situation calls for, with knock-on gains in
classroom readiness, problem solving, and social relationships
([CHADD](https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_10_12_Zones.pdf),
[zonesofregulation.com](https://zonesofregulation.com/)). The realistic
expectation, per the research so far, is more modest: studies show gains in
zone-concept knowledge and some short-lived reductions in off-task behavior, but
weak generalization to real-world situations and effects that fade when the
program ends
([ASAT review](https://asatonline.org/for-parents/becoming-a-savvy-consumer/zones-of-regulation-is-there-science-behind-that/)).
A fair summary: expect a genuinely useful shared vocabulary and check-in habit;
do not expect the curriculum alone to produce durable behavior change without
sustained practice and complementary supports.
