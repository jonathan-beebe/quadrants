# Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave

> **Energizes you vs. drains you:** a week of logged work shows what to do more
> of, where to grow, and what to shed.

Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave is a personal-reflection quadrant for auditing
your work by how it affects you. Its axes are **emotional charge** (does the
activity energize you or drain you?) and **disposition** (should you invest in
it or divest from it?). The quadrants: **Love** — activities that energize you,
where time flies and you feel strong; **Loathe** — activities that drain you and
that you dread or procrastinate on; **Learn** — activities and areas that are
teaching you something and are worth investing in; and **Leave** — activities,
habits, or commitments you should shed. A session runs as an observation
exercise rather than a one-shot brainstorm: over a period (a week of normal work
is the classic protocol), you note activities into the quadrants as you actually
experience them, then review the filled board to decide what to weave more of
into your work, what to reduce, hand off, or drop, and where to direct learning
effort. Unlike team retro formats, this one is primarily an individual
job-crafting tool, though it also works as a team-reflection round.

## When was it created?

There is no single creation event to report; this format is a modern blend of
two documented threads, and the specific Love/Loathe/Learn/Leave four-quadrant
combination has no identifiable published origin. Thread one: Marcus
Buckingham's two-column **"Loved It / Loathed It"** exercise from his
strengths-based work, in circulation by the late 2000s — it appears in an O, The
Oprah Magazine strengths piece from 2007 built on his work
([PDF](http://static.oprah.com/download/pdfs/omag/omag_200709_strength_tips.pdf),
via [wellfesto, 2013](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)),
alongside his book _Go Put Your Strengths to Work_ (2007), and was restated
through _StandOut_ (2011) and _Love + Work_ (Harvard Business Review Press,
2022). Thread two: the alliterative four-L retrospective pattern established by
Mary Gorman and Ellen Gottesdiener's **4Ls** (Liked / Learned / Lacked / Longed
For), first published on the EBG Consulting blog in 2010
([EBG Consulting](https://ebgconsulting.com/blog/the-4ls-a-retrospective-technique/))
and since catalogued widely ([Retromat #78](https://retromat.org/en/?id=78),
[Atlassian Team Playbook](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/4-ls-retrospective-technique)).
Love/Loathe/Learn/Leave-style quadrants appear in coaching and productivity
material from the 2010s onward as a fusion of these threads.

## Who created it?

No individual can honestly be credited with this exact four-quadrant format. Its
clearest intellectual parents are **Marcus Buckingham**, whose Loved It/Loathed
It listing exercise supplies the energize/drain axis and the underlying theory —
a strength is defined by how an activity makes you feel (anticipation, flow,
fulfillment), not by competence
([Awesome Office interview with Buckingham](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/))
— and **Mary Gorman and Ellen Gottesdiener**, whose 2010 4Ls retrospective
established the four-L reflection-board pattern that Learn/Leave-style variants
riff on
([EBG Consulting](https://ebgconsulting.com/blog/the-4ls-a-retrospective-technique/);
attribution corroborated by
[TeamRetro](https://www.teamretro.com/retrospectives/4ls-retrospective/) and
[Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/4-ls-retrospective-technique)).
Related quadrant variants (e.g. Loved/Loathed/Learned/Longed For) circulate in
the retro-template ecosystem without firm attribution; whoever first swapped in
"Leave" is unrecorded.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Buckingham's exercise attacks a measurement error in career development: people
(and the strengths-finder industry) equate strengths with what you are _good
at_, but competence at a draining activity is a trap — his definition is that
"strengths are not activities you're good at, they're activities that strengthen
you" ([Awesome Office](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/)). The
Loved/Loathed log replaces retrospective self-assessment (unreliable) with
in-the-moment observation of what actually energizes or depletes you, so you can
redesign your job around the "red threads" of love in it. Gorman and
Gottesdiener's 4Ls, meanwhile, aimed to make team retrospectives capture both
the factual and the **emotional** texture of an iteration in a fast, positive
frame. The Love/Loathe/Learn/Leave blend extends both with explicit action
categories: Learn directs growth investment, and Leave forces the often-avoided
decision to actually shed draining commitments rather than merely catalog them.

## Why should someone use it?

Use it when the question is not "how did the sprint go?" but "is my work
sustainable and pointed in the right direction?" It is one of the few retro
formats aimed at the individual: burnout risk shows up concretely as a heavy
Loathe column, misalignment shows up as a Love column with nothing from your
core role in it, stagnation shows up as an empty Learn quadrant. The
observation-log protocol makes it more truthful than a one-sitting reflection,
because entries are recorded as feelings occur rather than reconstructed
([wellfesto's account of the exercise](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)).
And the Leave quadrant gives it teeth that pure mood-mapping lacks — every
review ends with candidates for elimination, delegation, or renegotiation, not
just awareness.

## What is the expected outcome?

A concrete, evidence-based redesign of how you spend your working time: a list
of loved activities to deliberately weave more of into each week (Buckingham's
"pull on the red threads"), a list of loathed activities to reduce, reframe, or
hand off, chosen learning investments, and named commitments to drop.
Buckingham's claim for the underlying practice is that even modest rebalancing
toward loved activities measurably raises engagement and resilience — the goal
is not a job containing zero loathed tasks but one where love outweighs drain
([Awesome Office](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/)). Repeated
periodically, it functions as a personal continuous-improvement loop: the same
observational audit, run again, shows whether the Leave decisions were actually
executed and whether the Love column is growing.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

Thin, and that should be stated as fact. No peer-reviewed study validating the
Love/Loathe/Learn/Leave quadrant — or Buckingham's underlying Loved It/Loathed
It exercise — was found in accessible sources. Buckingham's claim that
rebalancing toward loved activities raises engagement and resilience is his own,
made without citation to published studies
([Awesome Office](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/)). What does
exist:

- **Adoption evidence.** The 4Ls pattern the format descends from is carried by
  major retrospective catalogues and vendors
  ([Retromat #78](https://retromat.org/en/?id=78),
  [Atlassian Team Playbook](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/4-ls-retrospective-technique),
  [TeamRetro](https://www.teamretro.com/retrospectives/4ls-retrospective/)) —
  evidence of sustained practical use, not of measured outcomes.
- **Design rationale.** The week-long real-time log captures feelings as they
  occur instead of reconstructing them afterward
  ([wellfesto](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)); no
  controlled trial comparing this protocol to alternatives was located.
- **Adjacent academic grounding.** The framework's premise — that employees can
  redesign the task and relational boundaries of their own jobs, with benefits
  to meaning and well-being — is the subject of the peer-reviewed job-crafting
  literature founded by Wrzesniewski and Dutton's "Crafting a Job" (_Academy of
  Management Review_, 26(2), 2001;
  [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_crafting)). That literature
  supports job crafting generally; it does not test this instrument.

## How do you get into the right mindset?

No published priming questions exist for this format; the orientation it needs
is the mindset shift behind Buckingham's exercise — judge activities by how they
make you feel, not by how well you perform them: "strengths are not activities
you're good at, they're activities that strengthen you"
([Awesome Office](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/)). Set that
lens with a few questions before drawing the board:

- Which task did you put off this week, and which did you start before you meant
  to? Dread and anticipation are the signals you will be logging.
- Where are you competent but flat? Name one competence trap up front so "good
  at it" does not leak into Love.
- What would you drop tomorrow if no one objected? If nothing comes to mind,
  expect resistance at the Leave quadrant and plan for it.
- Can you log in the moment for a full week? The protocol's value is
  observation, not recollection — entries reconstructed at day's end defeat it
  ([wellfesto](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)).

## How does one use it properly?

The base protocol is Buckingham's, extended to four quadrants
([wellfesto](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)):

1. Draw the four quadrants — Love, Loathe, Learn, Leave — on a sheet or note you
   can keep with you.
2. Carry it for one week of normal work. Log activities in real time, at the
   moment you feel the pull or the drain — not in an end-of-day recap.
3. Write specific activities, not roles or projects: "editing the weekly
   report," not "communication." The finer the grain, the more actionable the
   review.
4. At week's end, review the board. Pick three concrete ways to weave more
   Love-quadrant activities into a normal week.
5. For each Loathe item, choose a disposition: reduce it, reframe it, hand it
   off — or move it to Leave.
6. Name your Learn investments (what you will study, practice, or seek exposure
   to) and your Leave commitments (what you will drop, delegate, or renegotiate,
   and by when).
7. Enlist support — a manager or peer who can help make the changes stick.

Cadence: run it at times of transition and as an annual refresh
([wellfesto](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/)); each rerun
checks whether the previous Leave list was executed and whether the Love
quadrant is growing.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **4Ls (Liked / Learned / Lacked / Longed For)** — the direct sibling and
  structural parent; a team-retro format where this one points at the individual
  ([EBG Consulting](https://ebgconsulting.com/blog/the-4ls-a-retrospective-technique/)).
- **[Passion × Proficiency](./passion-proficiency.md)** — plots the same
  energize/drain signal against skill, making Buckingham's trap explicit: the
  high-proficiency, low-passion quadrant is exactly what lands in Loathe here.
  Run it after this audit to decide which Loathe items are competence traps.
- **[Energy Quadrants](./energy-quadrants.md)** — maps the same
  energizing/draining axis onto activities; useful as the mood-level companion
  to this framework's action-level sort.
- **[Start / Stop / Continue / Change](./start-stop-continue-change.md)** — the
  team-process counterpart: Love ≈ Continue, Leave ≈ Stop. Use it to convert a
  team-level reading of this board into process commitments.
- **[Keep / Problem / Try / Question](./keep-problem-try-question.md)** —
  another reflection board with explicit action categories; its Try quadrant
  plays the role Learn plays here.
- **[Importance × Satisfaction](./importance-satisfaction.md)** — cross-checks
  the Leave list: an activity you loathe but that scores high on importance
  needs redesign or delegation, not abandonment.
- **[Skill × Will](./skill-will.md)** — the manager's-eye view of the same
  person; a coach can pair it with an individual's Love/Loathe board to match
  assignments to energy, not just ability.
- **Job crafting** (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001) — the academic frame for what
  the review step produces: task, relational, and cognitive changes to one's own
  job ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_crafting)).

## What is the core insight?

A strength is what strengthens you, not what you are good at — so audit work by
observed emotional response and act on the result.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. The name "Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave" is a descriptive label
with no trademark registration found, and the format has no single owner to
assert one. Buckingham's books (_Go Put Your Strengths to Work_, _StandOut_,
_Love + Work_) are copyrighted works, but the Loved It/Loathed It exercise
itself is taught openly in articles and interviews
([wellfesto](https://wellfesto.com/2013/04/08/loved-itloathed-it/),
[Awesome Office](http://awesomeoffice.org/blog/marcus-buckingham/)); the 4Ls is
likewise published and cited openly with attribution to Gorman and Gottesdiener
([EBG Consulting](https://ebgconsulting.com/blog/the-4ls-a-retrospective-technique/)).
No licensing fee or restricted instrument is involved; any four-label reflection
quadrant over an energize/drain axis serves the same function.
