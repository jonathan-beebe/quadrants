# Eisenhower Matrix

> **Urgent vs. important:** untangles what presses for attention now from what
> advances long-term goals, so busyness stops passing for progress.

The Eisenhower Matrix is a 2×2 quadrant model for prioritizing tasks along two
axes: **urgency** (does this demand attention now?) and **importance** (does
this contribute to long-term goals?). Crossing the axes yields four quadrants
with an action attached to each: urgent and important — **Do First**; important
but not urgent — **Schedule**; urgent but not important — **Delegate**; neither
urgent nor important — **Eliminate**. It is used as a triage tool: list tasks,
judge each on both axes, place it in a quadrant, and take the quadrant's
prescribed action.

## When was it created?

The framework has a two-stage history, and no single creation date. The
urgent/important distinction was stated publicly by President Dwight D.
Eisenhower on **August 19, 1954**, in an
[address at the Second Assembly of the World Council of Churches](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-second-assembly-the-world-council-churches-evanston-illinois)
in Evanston, Illinois: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the
important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent."
The 2×2 grid built on that distinction appeared decades later — most
influentially as the time-management matrix in Stephen Covey's _The 7 Habits of
Highly Effective People_
([1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People)),
developed further in
[_First Things First_](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Things_First_(book)>)
(1994, with A. Roger Merrill and Rebecca R. Merrill). The label "Eisenhower
Matrix" was attached retroactively; there is no evidence Eisenhower himself drew
or used a matrix.

## Who created it?

The attribution is genuinely contested, and the honest answer is layered:

- **Eisenhower did not claim the insight as his own.** In the 1954 speech he
  explicitly attributed the words to an unnamed "former college president"
  ([American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-the-second-assembly-the-world-council-churches-evanston-illinois);
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management)).
  [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2014/05/09/urgent/) found
  no documented earlier use than Eisenhower's speech, and notes the popular
  "what is important is seldom urgent" phrasing appears to derive from imperfect
  recollections of that speech.
- **Stephen Covey** turned the distinction into the four-quadrant grid most
  people use today, in
  [_The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People)
  (1989, Habit 3: "Put First Things First"), pairing each quadrant with an
  action (do, plan/schedule, delegate, eliminate). Covey's book presents it as
  his time-management matrix, not as an "Eisenhower" tool; the Eisenhower name
  was attached by later popularizers.

So: the principle is Eisenhower-via-an-unnamed-source; the matrix form is most
credibly Covey's; the name is folk attribution.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Eisenhower's remark addressed the pull of crisis-driven work in leadership:
urgent problems crowd out the important ones. Covey formalized the grid to
attack the same failure mode in personal effectiveness — people spend their time
reacting to urgency (Quadrants I and III) and starve Quadrant II (important, not
urgent: planning, prevention, relationship-building, self-improvement), which is
where effectiveness actually compounds
([_7 Habits_, Habit 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People)).
The matrix exists to make the urgency trap visible and give each kind of task a
default action.

## Why should someone use it?

Because "sort by deadline" is the default human behavior, and it is a poor proxy
for value. The matrix forces two separate judgments — urgency and importance —
instead of one conflated one, which makes it obvious when you are busy with
things that don't matter. It is cheap (a sheet of paper suffices), fast enough
for daily triage, and prescriptive: every task lands in a quadrant that already
tells you what to do with it (do, schedule, delegate, eliminate)
([Wikipedia — Eisenhower method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management)).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the matrix shifts time from Quadrants III and IV
(urgent-but-unimportant busywork and pure time-wasters) into Quadrant II. The
expected outcome is fewer self-inflicted crises — because important work gets
scheduled before it becomes urgent — a shorter, more honest to-do list after
delegation and elimination, and steady progress on long-term goals rather than a
career of firefighting. Covey's claim is that Quadrant II investment is what
distinguishes effective people; the matrix is the mechanism for making that
investment deliberate
([_7 Habits_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People)).

## What is the hard evidence it works?

The evidence base is real but modest: small studies and practitioner surveys, no
large randomized trials.

- **A peer-reviewed quasi-experimental study** of 50 Grade 12 Accountancy,
  Business, and Management students (25 male, 25 female) found statistically
  significant pre-test/post-test improvement in time management after applying
  the matrix, across attitude toward time, prioritization of academic tasks, and
  school–family balance, with no significant gender difference
  ([Monsalve et al., Ascendens Asia](https://ojs.aaresearchindex.com/index.php/aasgbcpjmra/article/view/1152)).
- **A 2021 survey of 500 workers** by the UK-based Development Academy (Acuity
  Training) found the Eisenhower Matrix the most successful of the time
  management techniques surveyed: 100% of its users reported feeling their work
  under control 4–5 days per week
  ([Acuity Training](https://www.acuitytraining.co.uk/news-tips/time-management-statistics-research/);
  summarized by
  [TimeWatch](https://www.timewatch.com/blog/is-the-eisenhower-matrix-the-best-time-management-system/)).
  This is self-reported survey data, not a controlled comparison.
- **Academic work on the matrix's limits** exists alongside the supportive
  findings: researchers have proposed refinements
  ([the Sung Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343686873_The_Sung_Diagram_Revitalizing_the_Eisenhower_Matrix))
  and combined the matrix with the Saaty Analytical Hierarchy Process on the
  grounds that unweighted quadrant sorting alone is too coarse for complex
  prioritization
  ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333516142_Eisenhower_matrix_Saaty_AHP_Strong_actions_prioritization_Theoretical_literature_and_lessons_drawn_from_empirical_evidences)).
- **Documented critiques**: importance and urgency judgments are subjective and
  observer-dependent
  ([Pure Mind Journal](https://www.puremindjournal.com/blog/pros-and-cons-of-the-eisenhower-matrix)),
  the four-category sort oversimplifies and costs more effort than it appears
  ([t2informatik](https://t2informatik.de/en/smartpedia/eisenhower-matrix/)),
  and the framework fits poorly in fast-changing environments where new urgent
  tasks arrive continuously
  ([Voigt](https://stephan-voigt.com/blog/the-eisenhower-matrix-is-misleading-why-prioritization-is-broken)).

## How do you get into the right mindset?

The sort only works if urgency and importance are judged as separate properties,
against criteria fixed before anything gets plotted. Practitioners open with
questions that pull the two judgments apart:

- "Are your tasks really moving you forward or just keeping you busy?" — the
  opening question that frames the exercise as busyness versus progress
  ([Situational](https://situational.com/blog/the-eisenhower-matrix-explained/)).
- For urgency: "Does it have a real, near-term deadline or consequence?" Be
  strict — "I'd like this done soon" is not urgent
  ([Any.do](https://www.any.do/blog/the-eisenhower-matrix-how-to-prioritize-what-actually-matters/)).
- For importance: "Does this task genuinely contribute to an outcome you care
  about?"
  ([Any.do](https://www.any.do/blog/the-eisenhower-matrix-how-to-prioritize-what-actually-matters/)).
- Two coaching probes for after a first pass: "Which of these are genuinely
  time-sensitive, in the sense that something breaks if they don't happen this
  week?" and, pointing at Quadrant II, "What belongs here that isn't scheduled?"
  ([Tandem Coach](https://tandemcoach.co/coaching-tools/planning-eisenhower-priority-matrix/)).

A warm-up for groups: each participant lists their top 10 activities from the
past week, plots them, and totals the time per quadrant. Groups discover 60–80%
of their time sits in Quadrants I and III — a concrete view of why they feel
busy but not productive, and motivation for the sort that follows
([Kithindin](https://kithindin.com/eisenhower-matrix/)).

## How does one use it properly?

1. **List every task.** Inventory everything competing for your time —
   professional, personal, errands — before sorting anything
   ([ClickUp](https://clickup.com/blog/eisenhower-matrix/)).
2. **Judge each task on both axes separately.** Ask two questions: does this
   demand attention now (urgency)? Does this contribute to my long-term goals or
   values (importance)?
   ([Airtable](https://www.airtable.com/articles/eisenhower-matrix)).
3. **Place each task and take the quadrant's action.** Quadrant I (urgent +
   important): do first. Quadrant II (important, not urgent): schedule a
   concrete time. Quadrant III (urgent, not important): delegate or minimize.
   Quadrant IV (neither): eliminate
   ([FranklinCovey](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-3/)).
4. **Spend discretionary time in Quadrant II.** Covey's guidance: "The best
   return we can get on our time is to spend it on things which aren't urgent
   but are important — for example, planning, creative thinking, and learning
   and renewal"
   ([FranklinCovey](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-3/)).
5. **Review on a regular cadence.** Re-sort as tasks arrive and priorities shift
   — weekly for volatile task lists, monthly for stable ones, budgeting roughly
   30–50 minutes per review
   ([ClickUp](https://clickup.com/blog/eisenhower-matrix/);
   [ToolThinker](https://toolthinker.com/index.php/2024/11/30/the-eisenhower-matrix-framework-a-guide-to-prioritizing-tasks-for-maximum-productivity/)).

Keep granularity at the task level — items small enough to do, schedule,
delegate, or delete as a unit.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- [**Impact/Effort Matrix**](./impact-effort.md) — the nearest sibling in this
  library: same 2×2 shape, but its axes are value versus cost rather than value
  versus time pressure. Use Eisenhower to decide _when_ (or whether) something
  gets attention, Impact/Effort to decide _which_ candidate work is worth doing
  at all.
- [**Worry Matrix**](./worry-matrix.md) — applies quadrant triage to worries
  instead of tasks; a natural companion when the to-do list is driven by anxiety
  rather than deadlines.
- [**Importance × Satisfaction**](./importance-satisfaction.md) — shares the
  importance axis but crosses it with current satisfaction to find neglected
  life areas; useful upstream of Eisenhower for deciding what is "important" in
  the first place.
- [**Risk Matrix**](./risk-matrix.md) — the same prioritize-by-two-judgments
  move (likelihood × impact) applied to risks rather than tasks.
- **Pareto Principle (80/20 rule)** — Vilfredo Pareto's 1906 observation,
  popularized for management by Joseph Juran in the 1940s
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle)); commonly
  combined with the matrix to identify the few tasks worth calling important
  ([InLoox](https://www.inloox.com/company/blog/articles/effective-time-management-with-pareto-and-eisenhower/)).
- **MoSCoW method** — Dai Clegg's 1994 Must/Should/Could/Won't scheme; a sibling
  prioritization tool for project-scoping contexts
  ([Nowacki](https://medium.com/@nowacki.lukasz/moscow-method-vs-eisenhower-matrix-prioritization-of-tasks-in-the-project-372f8553c12a)).
- **ABCDE and POSEC methods, PICK chart** — alternative task-prioritization
  schemes covered alongside the Eisenhower method in surveys of the field
  ([IdeaScale](https://ideascale.com/blog/eisenhower-matrix-alternatives/);
  [Wikipedia — Time management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management)).
- **The 7 Habits framework** — the matrix is Habit 3 of Covey's larger system;
  Habits 1–2 (proactivity, "begin with the end in mind") supply the goals that
  make the importance axis meaningful
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_7_Habits_of_Highly_Effective_People)).

## What is the core insight?

Urgency and importance are independent properties, and the
important-but-not-urgent quadrant is where most neglected high-value work lives.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. The urgent/important distinction dates to a 1954 public speech and
is in the public domain; the "Eisenhower Matrix" name is folk attribution and
not trademarked as a framework name, and a general organizational scheme is not
patentable
([legal analysis](https://www.justanswer.com/business-law/a8xwe-use-chart-called-eisenhower-matrix-product.html)).
Two narrow constraints: a specific fixed illustration of the matrix can carry
its author's copyright (draw your own), and commercial use of the "Eisenhower"
name in ways implying endorsement could raise personality-rights issues with the
Eisenhower estate. Covey's books and FranklinCovey's "7 Habits" training
materials are copyrighted and licensed
([FranklinCovey](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-3/)),
but the underlying quadrant concept is not exclusively protected. Generic names
such as "urgency–importance matrix" or "four-quadrant prioritization" are safe
in any context.

## How do you use it generically?

Nothing branded is needed. The axes are plain words — urgency and importance —
and the quadrant actions (do, schedule, delegate, eliminate) are generic
descriptions, so labels can be renamed or reworded at will. Names free of the
Eisenhower attribution are in wide circulation: "Urgent Important Matrix"
([GroupMap](https://www.groupmap.com/templates/urgent-important-matrix/);
[Open Practice Library](https://openpracticelibrary.com/practice/eisenhower-box-or-urgent-important-matrix/)),
"Time Management Matrix"
([Todoist](https://www.todoist.com/productivity-methods/eisenhower-matrix)), and
"urgency–importance matrix" or "four-quadrant prioritization"
([legal analysis](https://www.justanswer.com/business-law/a8xwe-use-chart-called-eisenhower-matrix-product.html)).
Three things to avoid reproducing: another author's fixed illustration of the
grid — draw your own
([legal analysis](https://www.justanswer.com/business-law/a8xwe-use-chart-called-eisenhower-matrix-product.html));
Covey's book text and FranklinCovey's licensed "7 Habits" training materials
([FranklinCovey](https://www.franklincovey.com/courses/the-7-habits/habit-3/));
and commercial use of the "Eisenhower" name in ways implying endorsement by the
estate.
