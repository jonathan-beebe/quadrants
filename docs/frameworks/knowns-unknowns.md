# Knowns & Unknowns

> **Knowledge vs. awareness:** shows where confidence is earned, which questions
> to chase, and which blind spots only contingency can cover.

The Knowns & Unknowns matrix is a 2×2 quadrant model for classifying knowledge
and risk. Its axes are **Knowledge** (whether something is known or unknown) and
**Awareness** (whether we are aware of our knowledge state), yielding four
quadrants: **Known Knowns** (things we know we know), **Known Unknowns** (things
we know we don't know), **Unknown Knowns** (things we don't realize we know —
tacit knowledge or disavowed assumptions), and **Unknown Unknowns** (things we
don't know we don't know). It is used in risk management, intelligence analysis,
project planning, and strategy to make gaps in awareness explicit.

## When was it created?

There is no single creation date. The vocabulary of "known unknowns" and
"unknown unknowns" was in common use in the U.S. defense and aerospace
industries by the late 1960s — Hudson Drake of North American Rockwell
distinguished "known unknowns" from "unanticipated unknowns" in defense
contracting in 1968, Lt. Gen. William B. Bunker described the same two
categories of technical problems in weapons systems that year, and "unk-unks"
became industry shorthand that circulated inside NASA well before 2002
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). A 1979
British Columbia inquiry into uranium mining formally distinguished "known
unknowns" from "unknown unknowns" in risk assessment
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). The
conceptual ancestor of the full 2×2 grid is the
[Johari Window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window) (1955), which
crossed self-awareness against others' awareness. The framing became
world-famous on **February 12, 2002**, when U.S. Secretary of Defense Donald
Rumsfeld used it at a Department of Defense news briefing
([DoD transcript](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2002/020212/epf202.htm)).

## Who created it?

No single creator can honestly be named; the framework was **popularized**
rather than invented by Donald Rumsfeld. In his memoir, Rumsfeld credited
hearing "a variant of the phrase" from former NASA administrator **William
Graham** when they served together on the Commission to Assess the Ballistic
Missile Threat in the late 1990s, and the terminology was already common inside
NASA and defense procurement
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)).
Rumsfeld himself had used the terms publicly in a 2000 speech before the famous
2002 briefing. Note also that Rumsfeld named only three of the four quadrants;
the fourth, **unknown knowns**, was most prominently articulated afterward by
philosopher **Slavoj Žižek** as "that which one intentionally refuses to
acknowledge that one knows"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). Deeper
philosophical roots — the distinction between recognized and unrecognized
ignorance — go back at least to Socrates, al-Ghazali, and Thomas Aquinas.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

In its engineering and NASA lineage, the problem was **risk estimation under
incomplete information**: how to budget, schedule, and design for failures you
cannot yet name. Rumsfeld's 2002 usage addressed an intelligence-analysis
version of the same problem. Asked about the lack of evidence linking Iraq to
supplying weapons of mass destruction to terrorist groups, he answered that
"reports that say that something hasn't happened are always interesting to me,
because as we know, there are known knowns … known unknowns … but there are also
unknown unknowns — the ones we don't know we don't know," adding that
historically "it is the latter category that tend to be the difficult ones"
([DoD transcript, Feb. 12, 2002](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2002/020212/epf202.htm)).
The problem, in short: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and the
most dangerous risks are the ones outside your awareness entirely.

## Why should someone use it?

The matrix forces an explicit inventory of your epistemic state. Sorting facts,
assumptions, and risks into the four quadrants surfaces where confidence is
justified (known knowns), where targeted investigation will pay off (known
unknowns), where tacit knowledge or unexamined assumptions are hiding (unknown
knowns), and where humility, monitoring, and resilience are the only defenses
(unknown unknowns). It is simple enough to use in a meeting yet rigorous enough
to shape research plans, risk registers, and pre-mortems.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the exercise converts vague uncertainty into a structured map:
a shorter list of things wrongly treated as settled, a prioritized list of
answerable questions to go investigate, surfaced tacit knowledge and disavowed
assumptions, and explicit contingency margin for the unforeseeable. Teams that
use it should make fewer overconfident claims, direct investigation effort where
it actually reduces uncertainty, and build slack into plans for the "difficult
ones" — the unknown unknowns.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

No peer-reviewed controlled trials validating the framework's effectiveness were
found; its standing rests on sustained institutional use and expert commentary
rather than empirical study. The evidence that exists is observational: decades
of application in defense procurement, where Drake and Bunker documented its use
for managing technical uncertainty in weapons programs; aerospace engineering,
including metal-fatigue risk analysis; the 1979 British Columbia uranium mining
inquiry, which adopted it for formal risk classification; and analytical
chemistry, where mass spectrometry uses the known/unknown taxonomy for compound
identification
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). Expert
reception supports the underlying logic: economist John Quiggin wrote that
"although the language may be tortured, the basic point is both valid and
important," and columnist Mark Steyn called it "a brilliant distillation of
quite a complex matter"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). The
2003 Plain English Campaign "Foot in Mouth Award" criticized the phrasing, not
the substance.

## How do you get into the right mindset?

Arrive ready to demote your own certainties, not to defend them. Before plotting
anything, prime each quadrant with a question
([Developing Strategy Under Uncertainty](https://stephen.fm/known-unknown-matrix/)):

- **Known knowns** — is each entry "an actually provable fact," or "an
  assumption or belief hiding as a fact"?
- **Known unknowns** — which open questions carry the highest impact on this
  decision, and where would high-quality information come from?
- **Unknown knowns** — sleep on the decision; what does your intuition or gut
  tell you that no one has written down?
- **Unknown unknowns** — who outside the building — customers, adjacent fields,
  competitors — sees this differently?

For a group warm-up, brainstorm every question that remains unanswered about the
problem, then "give each participant three sticky dots and ask them to use these
to 'vote' for the most important questions that remain by sticking a dot on the
relevant note"
([Humanitarian Innovation Guide](https://higuide.elrha.org/methods/known-unknowns/)).
Voting before plotting anchors the session to the gaps that matter most.

## How does one use it properly?

1. **Frame a specific decision or domain** — a launch, a project plan, a risk
   review. The matrix maps the epistemic state of one question, not everything
   at once.
2. **Draw the four quadrants** and inventory each in turn. List known knowns
   (verified facts), known unknowns (named open questions), unknown knowns
   (assumptions and tacit knowledge — surfaced by asking each participant what
   they take for granted), and acknowledge the unknown-unknowns quadrant as
   structurally unfillable.
3. **Challenge the known knowns.** Demand a source or verification for each;
   move anything unverified into known unknowns.
4. **Prioritize the known unknowns** by impact and answerability, and assign
   owners to investigate the top items.
5. **Audit for unknown knowns as a group.** Cross-functional discussion and
   outside reviewers surface assumptions no individual would list alone.
6. **Budget for unknown unknowns.** Add schedule and cost contingency, build
   monitoring and early-warning signals, and plan for resilience rather than
   prediction — the defense-procurement lineage of the framework exists to fund
   failures not yet named
   ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)).
7. **Revisit at each major milestone.** No fixed cadence is prescribed; run it
   before major decisions, at project kickoff, and whenever new information
   moves items between quadrants.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **[Johari Window](./johari-window.md)** — the 1955 antecedent by Luft and
  Ingham; the same awareness logic applied to interpersonal knowledge (crossing
  what I know about myself against what others know). Using both distinguishes
  organizational blind spots from personal ones.
- **[Competence Ladder](./competence-ladder.md)** — the four stages of
  competence apply the known/unknown distinction to skill: conscious
  incompetence is a known unknown, unconscious competence is an unknown known. A
  learning-focused sibling of the same structure.
- **[Risk Matrix](./risk-matrix.md)** — scores risks you can already name by
  probability and impact. Knowns & Unknowns feeds it: known unknowns become
  risk-register entries; unknown unknowns explain why the register is never
  complete.
- **[Certainty × Agreement](./certainty-agreement.md)** — the Stacey matrix
  chooses a management approach based on how certain and agreed a problem is;
  Knowns & Unknowns supplies the honest certainty assessment that placement
  requires.
- **[Worry Matrix](./worry-matrix.md)** — sorts concerns by control; pairing it
  with this matrix separates unknowns worth investigating from unknowables worth
  releasing.
- **[SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md)** — a SWOT filled only with known knowns
  is overconfident; running Knowns & Unknowns first exposes which threats and
  weaknesses are still unknowns.
- **[One-Way / Two-Way Doors](./one-way-two-way-doors.md)** — reversibility is
  the practical hedge against unknown unknowns: prefer two-way doors when that
  quadrant looms large.

## What is the core insight?

The most dangerous gaps in a plan are not the questions you have listed but the
ones you do not yet know to ask, so mapping your awareness of your own ignorance
must precede managing it.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. The phrase "unknown unknowns" is not trademarked and the 2×2
methodology is not proprietary; the concepts are in the public domain and carry
no licensing restrictions on teaching or applying them. The framework goes by
several interchangeable names — "Knowns & Unknowns," "Known Unknowns," the
informal "Rumsfeld Matrix," and the defense-industry shorthand "unk-unks" — none
of them protected. The antecedent Johari Window (1955) is likewise in the public
domain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)). Rumsfeld's
2011 memoir _Known and Unknown_ is ordinary authorial use of the phrase, not an
exclusive claim
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)).
