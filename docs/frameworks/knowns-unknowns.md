# Knowns & Unknowns

The Knowns & Unknowns matrix is a 2×2 quadrant model for classifying knowledge
and risk. Its axes are **Knowledge** (whether something is known or unknown) and
**Awareness** (whether we are aware of our knowledge state), yielding four
quadrants: **Known Knowns** (things we know we know), **Known Unknowns** (things
we know we don't know), **Unknown Knowns** (things we don't realize we know —
tacit knowledge or disavowed assumptions), and **Unknown Unknowns** (things we
don't know we don't know). It is used in risk management, intelligence analysis,
project planning, and strategy to make gaps in awareness explicit.

## When was it created?

There is no single creation date. The vocabulary of "known unknowns" and
"unknown unknowns" was in common use in the U.S. defense and aerospace
industries by the late 1960s — a 1968 aerospace study referenced "unanticipated
unknowns," and the terms circulated inside NASA well before 2002
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). A 1979
British Columbia inquiry into uranium mining formally distinguished "known
unknowns" from "unknown unknowns" in risk assessment
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). The
conceptual ancestor of the full 2×2 grid is the
[Johari Window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window) (1955), which
crossed self-awareness against others' awareness. The framing became
world-famous on **February 12, 2002**, when U.S. Secretary of Defense Donald
Rumsfeld used it at a Department of Defense news briefing
([DoD transcript](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2002/020212/epf202.htm)).

## Who created it?

No single creator can honestly be named; the framework was **popularized**
rather than invented by Donald Rumsfeld. In his memoir, Rumsfeld credited
hearing "a variant of the phrase" from former NASA administrator **William
Graham** when they served together on the Commission to Assess the Ballistic
Missile Threat in the late 1990s, and the terminology was already common inside
NASA and defense procurement
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)).
Rumsfeld himself had used the terms publicly in a 2000 speech before the famous
2002 briefing. Note also that Rumsfeld named only three of the four quadrants;
the fourth, **unknown knowns**, was most prominently articulated afterward by
philosopher **Slavoj Žižek** as "that which one intentionally refuses to
acknowledge that one knows"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_are_unknown_unknowns)). Deeper
philosophical roots — the distinction between recognized and unrecognized
ignorance — go back at least to Socrates, al-Ghazali, and Thomas Aquinas.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

In its engineering and NASA lineage, the problem was **risk estimation under
incomplete information**: how to budget, schedule, and design for failures you
cannot yet name. Rumsfeld's 2002 usage addressed an intelligence-analysis
version of the same problem. Asked about the lack of evidence linking Iraq to
supplying weapons of mass destruction to terrorist groups, he answered that
"reports that say that something hasn't happened are always interesting to me,
because as we know, there are known knowns … known unknowns … but there are also
unknown unknowns — the ones we don't know we don't know," adding that
historically "it is the latter category that tend to be the difficult ones"
([DoD transcript, Feb. 12, 2002](https://usinfo.org/wf-archive/2002/020212/epf202.htm)).
The problem, in short: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and the
most dangerous risks are the ones outside your awareness entirely.

## Why should someone use it?

The matrix forces an explicit inventory of your epistemic state. Sorting facts,
assumptions, and risks into the four quadrants surfaces where confidence is
justified (known knowns), where targeted investigation will pay off (known
unknowns), where tacit knowledge or unexamined assumptions are hiding (unknown
knowns), and where humility, monitoring, and resilience are the only defenses
(unknown unknowns). It is simple enough to use in a meeting yet rigorous enough
to shape research plans, risk registers, and pre-mortems.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the exercise converts vague uncertainty into a structured map:
a shorter list of things wrongly treated as settled, a prioritized list of
answerable questions to go investigate, surfaced tacit knowledge and disavowed
assumptions, and explicit contingency margin for the unforeseeable. Teams that
use it should make fewer overconfident claims, direct investigation effort where
it actually reduces uncertainty, and build slack into plans for the "difficult
ones" — the unknown unknowns.
