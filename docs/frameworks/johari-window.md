# Johari Window

> **What you know of yourself vs. what others see:** self-awareness grows
> through two concrete acts — disclose what you hide, invite feedback on what
> you cannot see.

The Johari Window is a 2×2 quadrant model of interpersonal awareness. Its axes
are **Known to Self** and **Known to Others**, producing four quadrants (panes):
the **Open** area or Arena (behavior, feelings, and motivation known to self and
to others), the **Blind Spot** (known to others but not to self), the **Hidden**
area or Façade (known to self but not to others), and the **Unknown** (known to
neither). The panes are not fixed: self-disclosure shrinks the Hidden area,
soliciting feedback shrinks the Blind Spot, and both together enlarge the Open
area — the model's central mechanic for building self-awareness and trust
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).

## When was it created?

In **1955**. The model was devised while its authors were researching group
dynamics at the University of California, Los Angeles, and was first published
that year as "The Johari window, a graphic model of interpersonal awareness" in
the _Proceedings of the Western Training Laboratory in Group Development_,
issued by the UCLA Extension Office
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window);
[publication record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ingham-luft-thejohariwindowagraphicmodelofinterpersonalawareness-1955)).

## Who created it?

American psychologists **Joseph Luft** (1916–2014) and **Harrington "Harry"
Ingham** (1916–1995) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).
The name "Johari" is not a technical term — it is a blend of their first names,
**Jo**seph and **Hari**. The attribution here is unusually clean: unlike many
popular 2×2 models, the Johari Window has undisputed creators, a known
institution, and a dated primary publication.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Luft and Ingham were working in the human-relations training movement of the
1950s — laboratory-style group development sessions in the tradition of
T-groups, which grew out of Kurt Lewin's 1946 workshops at the National Training
Laboratories in Bethel, Maine
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_training)) — and needed a
way to represent how **awareness of behavior, feelings, and motivation is
unevenly distributed between a person and the group around them**
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)). People in groups act
on information others can see but they cannot (blind spots), and withhold
information others would need to understand them (the façade). The window gave
trainers and participants a simple graphic vocabulary for these mismatches and
for how disclosure and feedback change them — a model of the very dynamics their
training laboratories were designed to work on.

## Why should someone use it?

It turns the fuzzy goals of "self-awareness" and "trust-building" into two
concrete, practicable actions: **disclose more** (moving material from Hidden to
Open) and **invite feedback** (moving material from Blind Spot to Open). That
makes it a durable exercise for team formation, coaching, therapy, and feedback
conversations; it is widely used as a heuristic in self-help groups and
corporate settings ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).
It also gives groups a non-accusatory language: "that's in my blind spot" is
easier to say and hear than "you're wrong about me."

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended — iteratively, with genuine disclosure and solicited feedback —
the Open quadrant grows at the expense of the Hidden and Blind quadrants. The
expected result is greater individual self-awareness, deeper interpersonal
connection, better mutual understanding within a group, and occasionally
discoveries from the Unknown quadrant (capacities and motivations neither the
person nor the group had seen). In team settings the practical payoff is higher
trust and more effective communication, because more of each member's behavior
and motivation is common knowledge.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

**Direct empirical validation is absent.** No meta-analyses, randomized
controlled trials, or peer-reviewed effectiveness studies of the Johari Window
itself were located; Wikipedia describes it as a heuristic exercise used in
self-help groups and corporate settings without citing supporting research
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).

The closest evidence base is research on its parent methodology. Studies of
T-groups and sensitivity training — summarized in Irvin Yalom's _Theory and
Practice of Group Psychotherapy_ — found roughly **30% of participants reported
lasting benefit while 8% experienced equally lasting negative results**
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_training)). Carl Rogers
cautioned that participants "needed a solid ego to profit from it" given the
risk of intrusive, bullying group behavior
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_training)). The Johari
Window should be treated as an established practice tool with face validity, not
a validated instrument.

## How do you get into the right mindset?

The exercise asks for two uncomfortable acts — revealing something you hold
back, and hearing something you cannot see — so orient on both before plotting.
These questions follow from the model's own mechanics:

- **Open:** What do I assume everyone here already knows about how I work — and
  would they agree?
- **Hidden:** What am I withholding that this group would need to understand me?
- **Blind Spot:** When did feedback last surprise me, and did I defend or
  explore?
- **Trust check:** How much am I prepared to say — and to hear — in this room
  today?

A documented low-stakes warm-up is the adjective sort: pick the five or six
words that best describe you from a fixed list of 56, while peers pick from the
same list; the overlaps and differences seed the panes with concrete material
before anyone has to improvise a disclosure
([Kevan Davis's interactive Johari Window](https://kevan.org/johari)).

## How does one use it properly?

The creators published no formal step-by-step protocol; the practice below
follows the model's documented mechanics — disclosure and solicited feedback,
applied iteratively ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).

1. **Draw the window.** Label the axes **Known to Self** and **Known to
   Others**, and the four panes Open, Blind Spot, Hidden, Unknown.
2. **Map the Open pane.** Each participant lists traits, habits, and motivations
   they believe are common knowledge; the group confirms.
3. **Disclose.** Each participant shares one or more items from the Hidden pane
   that are relevant to the relationship or the work — moving them to Open.
   Disclosure is voluntary and sized to the trust in the room.
4. **Solicit feedback.** Each participant asks the group what they observe that
   the participant may not see — moving Blind Spot items to Open. Feedback is
   invited, specific, and about observable behavior.
5. **Repeat on a cadence.** Run the exercise at team formation and again at
   regular intervals (for example, each retrospective or coaching cycle). Each
   round should enlarge the Open pane; occasionally shared exploration surfaces
   material from the Unknown pane.

Keep granularity at the level of behaviors and working preferences in team
settings; reserve deeper personal material for therapeutic or coaching contexts
with an experienced facilitator.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

- **[Knowns & Unknowns](./knowns-unknowns.md)** — the direct structural
  descendant: it generalizes the Johari axes from interpersonal awareness (self
  vs. others) to epistemic awareness (knowledge vs. awareness of it). The Johari
  Unknown pane parallels "unknown unknowns"
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).
- **[Competence Ladder](./competence-ladder.md)** — crosses competence with
  consciousness of it; pairing the two locates skill blind spots (unconscious
  incompetence) that others' feedback can reveal.
- **[OK Corral](./ok-corral.md)** — a transactional-analysis 2×2 of
  interpersonal stance; it describes the attitude ("I'm OK, you're OK") that
  makes Johari-style disclosure and feedback safe.
- **[Start / Stop / Continue / Change](./start-stop-continue-change.md)** — a
  concrete feedback format that operationalizes the Blind-Spot-to-Open move in
  team retrospectives.
- **[SWOT Analysis](./swot-analysis.md)** — an organizational analogue:
  weaknesses visible to outsiders but not insiders are institutional blind
  spots.
- **Shadow (Jungian psychology)** — the unrecognized aspects of the psyche; a
  depth-psychology parallel to the Blind and Unknown panes
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)).
- **T-groups / sensitivity training and encounter groups** — the parent
  methodology (Lewin's National Training Laboratories) and Carl Rogers's
  extension of it, the settings the window was built to serve
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_training)).

## What is the core insight?

Self-awareness is not a private achievement — part of who you are is visible
only to others, so the Open area can grow only through the twin acts of
disclosing what you hide and inviting feedback on what you cannot see.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

**Free to use.** No trademark on the name "Johari" and no copyright restrictions
on the 2×2 diagram or the underlying concept were identified; the model is used
freely in academic, corporate, and training contexts
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window)). The primary source
is a 1955 UCLA Extension Office technical report with no documented licensing
terms
([publication record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ingham-luft-thejohariwindowagraphicmodelofinterpersonalawareness-1955)).
No generic alternative name is needed.
