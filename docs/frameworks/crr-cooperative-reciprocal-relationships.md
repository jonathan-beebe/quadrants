# CRR — Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships

> **Drifting vs. choosing:** which relationships to keep, renegotiate, end, or
> add so your needs get met on purpose, not by chance.

Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships (CRR) is a relationship-inventory practice
created and taught by Dr. Robert A. Glover, author of _No More Mr. Nice Guy_.
Its unit of analysis is the full set of relationships and resources a person
uses to get their needs met. Each qualifies on two criteria: **cooperative**
(everyone involved wants to be there) and **reciprocal** (both sides get
something of value). Glover's original form is a three-page mapping exercise —
current relationships, desired relationships, and relationships that fail one of
the two criteria
([Glover, "How to get your needs met," 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)).
This app renders it as a 2×2 retrospective — **Is Working** (current, healthy),
**Desire** (missing, wanted), **Renegotiate** (current, fixable terms), and
**Get Rid Of** (current, failed) — an adaptation of Glover's three pages, with
page three split by remedy.

## When was it created?

The documented publication is Glover's video "How to get your needs met," posted
to his channel on August 19, 2022, in which he presents the practice by name and
in full ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). In it he
states he has been teaching the process in his workshops "to men and women for
several years," placing its origin in the late 2010s at the latest. No earlier
dated publication of the practice was found.

## Who created it?

**Dr. Robert A. Glover**, the therapist and coach behind _No More Mr. Nice Guy_,
listed on Wikipedia as "a 2000 self-help book by Robert A. Glover"
([Wikipedia disambiguation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_More_Mr._Nice_Guy)).
He teaches CRR through his workshops, courses, and channel; his resources live
at [drglover.com](https://drglover.com). In the video he credits M. Scott Peck's
_The Road Less Traveled_ for the underlying premise: parents who meet a child's
needs "in timely, judicious ways" teach the child that they are lovable and that
their needs matter ([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ);
[The Road Less Traveled — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Road_Less_Traveled)).
Neither Glover nor the practice has a standalone Wikipedia article. The acronym
collides with **CRR Global**, the coaching company behind ORSC; that company's
"CRR" stood for "Center for Right Relationship," and no connection to Glover's
practice exists
([CRR Global — Our Story](https://crrglobal.com/about/our-story/)).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Glover's premise: "you cannot get all of your needs met on your own, but you are
responsible for surrounding yourself with people and resources that help you get
your needs met in a predictable and consistent way"
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). The practice
targets two failure modes. People whose needs were poorly met in childhood
under-build their support network as adults, and people default to
"codependent-style relationships" — one-sided arrangements that are neither
chosen nor mutual. CRR replaces both with what Glover calls "conscious
bucket-filling relationships": a deliberately built, regularly pruned network in
which every connection is wanted by all parties and delivers value in both
directions.

## Why should someone use it?

- The two criteria give a concrete test for any relationship: does everyone want
  to be here, and do both sides get value?
- Mapping every current relationship makes an invisible support network visible;
  Glover reports the map alone prompts reconnection with neglected friendships
  ([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)).
- The four categories — **People**, **Professionals**, **Packs** (groups), and
  **Practices** (daily self-care) — widen "relationships" beyond friends and
  family to the full set of resources that meet needs.
- Naming missing relationships converts vague loneliness into specific,
  communicable wants; Glover's own examples (a men's group, an accountant, a
  financial advisor) were filled by stating them at workshops.
- Reviewing the map surfaces relationships to renegotiate or end, making room
  for healthier ones.

## What is the expected outcome?

Glover's stated outcome is building "your good family and your all-star team": a
large, active network across all four categories that fills needs predictably.
His reported results are personal: joining a men's program that took him "from
1.5 guy friends to well over 25," a financial advisor and accountant found
through the practice, and an exercise-accountability group whose check-in
prompted him back into daily exercise
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). Run on its
twice-yearly cadence, the practice's expected output is a current map of the
network, a shortlist of relationships to add, and explicit decisions on
relationships to renegotiate or end.

## What is the hard evidence it works?

None. No peer-reviewed study, outcome data, or published evaluation of the CRR
practice exists; the evidence Glover offers in the source video is his own
account and workshop anecdotes
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). The premise he
cites — that early needs-meeting shapes felt lovability — comes from Peck's _The
Road Less Traveled_, a clinical argument rather than a controlled finding
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Road_Less_Traveled)). Structured
relationship review as a category has adjacent support in the couples-therapy
literature (Gottman Method, EFT, IBCT effectiveness data), but that evidence
does not test this practice
([Couples therapy — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couples_therapy)).

## How do you get into the right mindset?

Glover publishes no warm-up questions; the exercise's own opening move is the
orientation — before mapping anyone else, draw yourself in the middle of the
page holding a bucket, the needs you are trying to fill
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). His two criteria
double as per-relationship judging questions: _does everyone involved want to be
here?_ (cooperative) and _do both sides get something of value?_ (reciprocal)
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)). Before plotting,
sit with:

- What needs are in your bucket right now, and which are you trying to meet
  entirely on your own?
- Which of your relationships did you consciously choose, and which did you
  drift into or inherit?
- Where does "relationship" stop for you — and what changes if professionals,
  groups, and daily practices count too?
- What are you tolerating in a relationship because renegotiating or ending it
  feels harder than staying?

## How does one use it properly?

Glover's published protocol
([Glover, 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HXETLDSDxTQ)):

1. **Materials.** Three large sheets of poster board or butcher paper and
   colored markers.
2. **Page one — current CRRs.** Draw yourself in the middle with a bucket (the
   needs to fill). Around you, draw one circle per current cooperative
   reciprocal relationship, across four categories: **People** (friends, family,
   associates), **Professionals** (doctor, dentist, trainer, therapist),
   **Packs** (church, team, book club, 12-step group, mastermind), and
   **Practices** (exercise, prayer, meditation, time in nature). Spend several
   days on it; color-code categories; aim high — Glover's benchmark is a hundred
   circles. Share the page with others to surface forgotten relationships.
3. **Page two — desired CRRs.** Same layout, but circles are relationships to
   add. Communicate these wants; Glover filled his own page-two entries by
   stating them at workshops. Update the page as wants change.
4. **Page three — failed CRRs.** Circles are current relationships that are not
   cooperative or not reciprocal. Each gets one of three actions: discuss,
   renegotiate, or end — making room for new relationships.
5. **Cadence.** Repeat the full practice at least twice a year.

In this app's quadrant form: **Is Working** holds page one, **Desire** holds
page two, and page three splits into **Renegotiate** (new terms could fix it)
and **Get Rid Of** (it ends). The bucket, categories, and cadence carry over.

## How does it relate to other frameworks?

CRR belongs to the retrospective family, scoped to a person's relationship
network. [Start / Stop / Continue / Change](./start-stop-continue-change.md) is
the closest structural sibling — Is Working maps to Continue, Desire to Start,
Get Rid Of to Stop, Renegotiate to Change — applied to team process.
[Love / Loathe / Learn / Leave](./love-loathe-learn-leave.md) applies the same
sorting to one's job, and
[Keep / Problem / Try / Question](./keep-problem-try-question.md) to team
practice. Frameworks that reinforce it:
[Attachment Styles](./attachment-styles.md) explains the relating patterns —
Glover's "codependent-style relationships" — that CRR is built to replace; the
[OK Corral](./ok-corral.md) names the I'm-OK-You're-OK stance a cooperative,
reciprocal relationship runs on;
[Importance × Satisfaction](./importance-satisfaction.md) ranks which Desire and
Renegotiate items matter most; and the [Worry Matrix](./worry-matrix.md)
separates relationship concerns you can act on from ones you can only accept.
Outside this library, the practice's stated root is Peck's _The Road Less
Traveled_ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Road_Less_Traveled)),
and Glover's _No More Mr. Nice Guy_ supplies its surrounding theory of needs and
covert one-sidedness
([Wikipedia disambiguation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_More_Mr._Nice_Guy)).

## What is the core insight?

You cannot get all of your needs met on your own; you are responsible for
building — and pruning — a network of relationships that are both cooperative
and reciprocal.

## What is its intellectual-property status?

Free to use. No trademark on "Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships" or "CRR" in
this sense was found; Glover's video, books, and course materials are
copyrighted, and the practice itself is a generic mapping exercise anyone may
run. The unrelated coaching company CRR Global holds trademarks on **ORSC™** and
**CRR Global™** ([CRR Global](https://crrglobal.com/)) — avoid implying a
connection to that brand. No Wikipedia article exists for the practice, for
Glover, or for his book (the book appears on a disambiguation page only).
