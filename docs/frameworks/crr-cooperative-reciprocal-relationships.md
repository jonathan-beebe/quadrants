# CRR — Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships

The CRR quadrant is a four-category model for running a retrospective on a
relationship — romantic, professional, or team. Participants sort aspects of the
relationship into four boxes: **Is Working** (what to appreciate and keep),
**Desire** (what is wanted but missing), **Get Rid Of** (what to stop or
remove), and **Renegotiate** (existing agreements or patterns that need to be
revisited together). Unlike the other frameworks in this collection, this one
has no documented published origin under this name: the provenance of the
specific quadrant, and even of the expansion "Cooperative Reciprocal
Relationships," could not be verified against any primary source. What follows
reports the most plausible lineage and is explicit about where the evidence
stops.

## When was it created?

Unknown. No published source documenting a quadrant with these four labels —
under the name "CRR," "Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships," or otherwise — was
found in books, coaching curricula, or the retrospective-facilitation literature
(searched July 2026). The most likely parent tradition, relationship systems
coaching, has a documented timeline: Faith Fuller and Marita Fridjhon ran the
first Organization and Relationship Systems Coaching (ORSC) course in June 2002
under the Coaches Training Institute banner, founded the **Center for Right
Relationship** in January 2007, and renamed the company CRR Global in July 2010
([CRR Global — Our Story](https://crrglobal.com/about/our-story/)). If the
quadrant derives from that tradition, it would date from the 2000s at the
earliest; but that derivation is inference, not documented fact.

## Who created it?

Unknown. The acronym "CRR" strongly suggests a connection to
[CRR Global](https://crrglobal.com/about/our-story/), the coaching company
founded by **Faith Fuller** (a psychologist with a background in couples and
organizational work) and **Marita Fridjhon** (a clinical social worker and
family-systems therapist), whose work is rooted in systems thinking and Arnold
Mindell's Process Work ([CRR UK](https://www.crruk.com/who-we-are/)). However,
in that company's name CRR originally stood for "**Center for Right
Relationship**" — not "Cooperative Reciprocal Relationships"
([CRR Global — Our Story](https://crrglobal.com/about/our-story/)). The
expansion used in this app's template appears to be either a later
reinterpretation, a folk backronym, or an independent coinage; no source using
it was found. Fuller and Fridjhon are co-creators of ORSC and of the
Relationship Systems Intelligence concept
([CRR Global — About ORSC](https://crrglobal.com/about-orsc/)), and could
plausibly be the ultimate source of a relationship-review exercise like this
one, but they cannot be honestly credited with this specific quadrant on current
evidence.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Speaking to the parent tradition, since the quadrant's own intent is
undocumented: ORSC was built on the insight that coaching individuals is not
enough — relationships themselves are systems with their own dynamics, and "by
nurturing the network of relationship between us, we can transform systems both
large and small"
([CRR Global — Our Story](https://crrglobal.com/about/our-story/)). A signature
ORSC practice, the **Designed Alliance** (Designed Team/Partnership Alliance),
has partners explicitly co-create the ground rules of their relationship rather
than leaving them implicit
([CRR Global — Fundamentals](https://crrglobal.com/course/fundamentals/)). The
CRR quadrant addresses the natural follow-on problem: relationships drift,
implicit agreements go stale, and resentments accumulate without a structured
occasion to name what works, what is missing, what should end, and what needs
renegotiating. It brings the agile retrospective's cadence of honest review
(compare "keep / drop / add"-style formats) to the relationship itself.

## Why should someone use it?

Most relationship friction is never discussed because there is no safe, neutral
container for discussing it. A quadrant gives both parties the same four prompts
at the same time, which does several useful things: it balances appreciation (Is
Working) against complaint, so the review doesn't become an attack; it separates
_removal_ (Get Rid Of) from _revision_ (Renegotiate), acknowledging that some
patterns should end while others just need new terms; and the Desire quadrant
legitimizes asking for things that were never part of the relationship's
original implicit contract. The framing treats agreements as renegotiable by
design — the core stance of the Designed Alliance tradition
([CRR Global — Fundamentals](https://crrglobal.com/course/fundamentals/)) —
rather than as fixed terms that can only be silently endured or dramatically
broken.

## What is the expected outcome?

A jointly held, explicit picture of the relationship's current state and a
concrete set of agreements going forward: reinforced continuation of what both
parties value, one or more named desires converted into requests, specific
behaviors or patterns agreed to stop, and stale agreements consciously
renegotiated on new terms. Done recurrently — as a retrospective rather than a
one-time intervention — the expected outcome is a relationship whose terms stay
deliberately designed instead of silently drifting, which is the stated aim of
the relationship-systems tradition it most likely descends from
([CRR Global — What is ORSC](https://crrglobal.com/what-is-orsc/)).
