# Certainty × Agreement

The Certainty × Agreement matrix — widely known as the **Stacey Matrix** — is a
2×2 quadrant model for choosing how to approach a piece of work based on its
context. Its axes are **Certainty** (how close the situation is to
cause-and-effect being knowable, e.g. from past experience) and **Agreement**
(how much the people involved agree on what should be done). Where certainty and
agreement are both high, work is simple: **Plan** it. High agreement but low
certainty calls for **Experiment**; high certainty but low agreement calls for
**Negotiate**; and far from both lies chaos, where the priority is to
**Stabilize**. Stacey's original diagram was drawn as a continuum with regions
(from rational decision-making through a "zone of complexity" to chaos) rather
than a strict 2×2; the quadrant form and zone names above are a later,
widely-used adaptation
([Praxis Framework](https://www.praxisframework.org/en/library/stacey-matrix)).

## When was it created?

The diagram was developed in the **mid-1990s**. Stacey's textbook _Strategic
Management and Organisational Dynamics_ was first published in 1993 and is now
in its 7th edition
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Douglas_Stacey)); the
agreement-and-certainty diagram appeared during his early "systemic" phase of
work in the 1990s and spread from the textbook into management, healthcare, and
— much later — Agile training materials
([Informal Coalitions](https://informalcoalitions.typepad.com/informal_coalitions/2009/08/staceys-certainty-agreement-matrix-and-levels-of-complexity.html)).

## Who created it?

**Ralph Douglas Stacey** (1942–2021), a British organizational theorist,
complexity researcher, and Professor of Management at Hertfordshire Business
School, University of Hertfordshire
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Douglas_Stacey)). He was a
pioneer in applying the complexity sciences to human organizations
([Mowles, obituary, 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/05333164221075533)).
Important caveat: **Stacey later disowned the matrix.** In his later "responsive
processes" phase he dropped the diagram from his own work and argued against its
use, "because it is easily interpreted in a way that collapses the paradox of
certainty and uncertainty and so sustains the dominant discourse on management
while using an alternative jargon of complexity"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Douglas_Stacey); see also
[The Cynefin Co, "Stacey Unresolved"](https://thecynefin.co/stacey-unresolved/)).
Most versions in circulation today — including four-zone quadrant renderings
with labels like "complicated" and "complex" — are third-party adaptations that
Stacey did not endorse.

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Stacey was attacking the assumption, dominant in strategic management, that
organizations can be run by rational, linear planning alone — that managers can
always predict outcomes and align stakeholders in advance. His contingency
diagram expressed the idea that "appropriate forms of decision-making and
control depend upon the nature of the change situations faced"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Douglas_Stacey)): traditional
planning and control work only close to certainty and agreement, while most
interesting organizational life happens in the zone of complexity, where
cause-and-effect is unclear and people disagree, and where different — emergent,
exploratory, political — ways of working are needed.

## Why should someone use it?

It is a fast, shared diagnostic for **matching method to situation**. Before
defaulting to a detailed plan, a team can ask two questions — how certain are we
about cause and effect, and how much do we agree on what to do? — and pick an
approach accordingly: plan and execute when both are high, run experiments and
iterate when the path is uncertain, invest in negotiation and coalition-building
when the conflict is about goals rather than facts, and act to stabilize first
when far from both. It gives teams a vocabulary for saying "this is not a
planning problem" without that sounding like an excuse. Use it with the caveat
its own author raised: contexts shift continuously, and the matrix is a
conversation-starter, not a decision algorithm.

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the matrix produces fewer category errors in how work is
approached: less waterfall-style planning applied to genuinely complex or
contested problems, less needless experimentation on well-understood ones, and
earlier recognition of situations where the real work is political (building
agreement) rather than technical (building certainty). The broader expected
outcome is a team that consciously selects its management approach per situation
instead of applying one habitual style everywhere — while staying alert to
Stacey's own warning that neatly categorizing a situation can create a false
sense of control.
