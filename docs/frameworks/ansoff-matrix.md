# Ansoff Matrix

The Ansoff Matrix (also called the Product–Market Growth Matrix) is a 2×2 model
of growth strategy whose axes are **products** (existing vs. new) and
**markets** (existing vs. new). The four resulting strategies carry escalating
risk: **Market Penetration** (existing products, existing markets — sell more to
the customers you have), **Market Development** (existing products, new
markets), **Product Development** (new products, existing markets), and
**Diversification** (new products, new markets — the highest-risk quadrant)
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansoff_matrix)).

## When was it created?

The framework originates in Igor Ansoff's article
["Strategies for Diversification"](https://archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr),
published in _Harvard Business Review_, September–October 1957, pp. 113–124. One
honest nuance: the 1957 article laid out the four product–market strategy
combinations, but the tidy 2×2 diagram everyone now calls "the Ansoff Matrix" is
a later stylization — Ansoff continued developing the product–market "growth
vector" idea, notably in his 1965 book _Corporate Strategy_, and the modern
quadrant rendering and name became standard through subsequent textbooks rather
than appearing fully formed in 1957.

## Who created it?

**H. Igor Ansoff** (1918–2002), a Russian-American applied mathematician and
business manager often called the father of strategic management. He developed
the framework while working in corporate planning (he had come from the RAND
Corporation and Lockheed) and published it in HBR in 1957
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansoff_matrix)). Attribution here is
unusually clean: the matrix bears its actual creator's name, and the primary
source is freely readable at the
[Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr).

## What problem were they trying to solve?

Postwar American firms faced a growth question: demand in their core markets
would not expand forever, and managers were diversifying haphazardly — often by
acquisition — without a way to reason about which growth moves fit the firm.
Ansoff's article gave executives a **systematic vocabulary for growth
alternatives**, distinguishing diversification (new products for new markets)
from the three less radical moves, and arguing that each combination demands
different competences and carries different risk. The framework let a management
team ask "which growth vector are we actually choosing, and are we equipped for
it?" instead of treating all growth as one undifferentiated ambition
([Ansoff, 1957](https://archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr)).

## Why should someone use it?

- It forces an explicit choice among **four distinct growth paths**, each with
  its own risk profile — penetration being the safest, diversification the
  riskiest — so risk is discussed up front rather than discovered later
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansoff_matrix)).
- It exposes the **hidden cost of novelty**: every step away from existing
  products and existing customers discards hard-won knowledge, and the matrix
  makes that trade visible on two axes.
- It is an effective **portfolio conversation tool**: plotting current
  initiatives on the grid quickly shows whether a growth plan is dangerously
  concentrated in one quadrant (all penetration, or all diversification).

## What is the expected outcome?

Used as intended, the outcome is a **deliberate, risk-aware growth strategy**:
the organization knows which quadrant each growth initiative occupies, has
matched the initiative to the capabilities it actually requires, and holds a mix
of moves whose combined risk it can afford. In practice that means fewer
accidental diversifications, clearer resourcing (a product-development bet is
staffed and measured differently from a market-development bet), and a shared
management understanding of where next year's revenue is supposed to come from —
and how likely it is to arrive.
